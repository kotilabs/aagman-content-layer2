## Instagram Carousel — The Four Kinds of Money Behind India's Strategic Build-Out

**In one August week, India proposed an ₹80,000 crore industrial complex, rented two military drones, and celebrated a homegrown chip. The financing behind each one is the story.**

---

### Slide 1

Start in Odisha. Karan Adani met the chief minister with a three-part roadmap: a bauxite-to-aluminium complex anchored by Dhamra port, two 3,000 MW nuclear plants, and a coal-to-chemicals facility in Sundargarh estimated at ₹80,000 crore. Proposed, not sanctioned. No MoU text, no board approval, no clearance filings are public. Two 3,000 MW nuclear plants would be a scale no private Indian group has ever built.

---

### Slide 2

To read an announcement like that, follow the equity. Adani has tapped public markets five times in eighteen months: a ₹25,000 crore rights issue in 2025, a ₹15,000 crore Adani Enterprises QIP upsized on demand this July, ₹8,500 crore and ₹3,500 crore at Adani Energy Solutions in May and July, and board approval for up to ₹15,000 crore more at Adani Power. Over ₹50,000 crore raised or approved since 2025. Odisha lands on top of this pipeline, not instead of it.

---

### Slide 3

India's industrial history is littered with MoU-stage mega-projects that never reached financial close. The gates are land, clearances and timelines, in that order. Announcement volume is not capex. What separates this cycle is that the money being raised is dated and disclosed, which makes the gap between proposal and paper trail measurable for the first time.

---

### Slide 4

The government runs its own version of the same problem. On August 17, the Ministry of Defence signed a ₹1,943 crore, 30-month lease with General Atomics for two MQ-9B Sea Guardian drones. The Navy already flies two leased MQ-9Bs and waits on a $3.99 billion order for 31, signed back in October 2024. Divide it out: roughly ₹65 crore a month, for the pair, to rent surveillance while the owned fleet is pending.

---

### Slide 5

That lease works as an instrument, not procurement. It keeps capability on station while the purchase grinds through, and it converts a capital problem into an operating expense. Useful, expensive, and quietly renewable.

---

### Slide 6

Chennai offers the counterexample. In February 2026, a startup called Aheesa taped out VIHAAN-I, a fully indigenous 28nm broadband chip built on C-DAC's VEGA RISC-V core, funded under MeitY's Design Linked Incentive scheme. Twenty-eight nanometres is a node old enough to be cheap and new enough to matter; foundry capacity is abundant. India bought the design margin without the fab capex.

---

### Slide 7

Space has no instrument at all. India's estimated cost to orbit in 2025 was $13,302 per kg, the highest among major spacefaring nations. Europe manages $9,897, the US $3,225. India's heaviest communication satellite, the 4,700 kg GSAT-N2, flew on a Falcon 9. Per-kg cost is fixed costs divided by cadence. India ran five launches in 2025.

---

### Slide 8

Four instruments, four failure modes. Equity-funded conglomerate capex breaks on leverage if execution slips. Leased defence breaks on dependence; thirty months assumes the owned fleet arrives. Subsidised design breaks on fragmentation; one tapeout is not a pipeline. Launch economics break on absence: no heavy vehicle, no cadence, no cost curve to climb.

---

### Slide 9

The resolution is procedural. For Odisha, watch whether a signed MoU with land and timelines arrives before the next equity raise; the order tells you which is funding which. For the drones, watch whether the 31-aircraft delivery schedule holds. For silicon, watch for a second tapeout from a second company. For space, watch for a launch manifest that grows. Each instrument reports its own health in documents, not press releases.

---

### Slide 10

The build-out is real, and the instruments carrying it are young. Read the paper trail: dated, disclosed, filed. India is learning to finance strategy four ways at once, and each way gets tested separately.
