# The ₹349 Floor: How Indian Telecom Turned a Price War Into Pricing Power

## Three companies now set the price of a billion phone connections, and the one institution that could stop them collects on both sides of the bill.

Imagine a street with ten tea stalls. A new stall opens, bankrolled by the richest family in town, and gives the tea away free. Within three years, seven stalls are gone, two have merged, and the street has three stalls plus a sleepy municipal canteen. Then prices start rising, always together, always within days of each other. But the cleverest move is never the headline price: the smallest cup keeps vanishing, so the cheapest way to drink on this street costs a little more each year, and no one ever has to announce a hike.

That street is Indian telecom, and the vanishing cup is the ₹299 prepaid plan. What is happening now is not a tariff story. It is the final phase of a consolidation that collapsed ten-plus operators into three, and the mechanism to watch is not the headline hike percentage. It is the entry-price ratchet: the floor under what it costs to keep a phone number alive, which moves in one direction only. The real question is whether the state, which happens to be the weakest operator's largest creditor, will let it keep turning.

## What just happened to India's cheapest phone plan?

In early August 2026, Airtel quietly deleted its ₹299 prepaid plan from its website and app. The ₹349 pack — unlimited calls, 2GB a day, 100 SMS a day, 28 days — is now the cheapest way to keep an Airtel SIM active, a 17% jump at the floor ([Gadgets360, 5 August 2026](https://www.gadgets360.com/telecom/news/airtel-rs-299-579-619-prepaid-plans-discontinued-price-in-india-benefits-offers-11868444)). Airtel had raised that same floor from ₹249 to ₹299 only a year earlier.

The floor move is the advance guard of a broader cycle. Trade press now expects Jio, Airtel and Vodafone Idea to raise prepaid tariffs by 12–15% within roughly the next hundred days, with postpaid to follow ([Trak.in, 6 August 2026](https://trak.in/stories/airtel-jio-vi-expected-to-raise-tariff-by-12-15-in-next-100-days/); [Cashify, 7 July 2026](https://www.cashify.in/news/jio-airtel-vi-tariff-hike-2026-prepaid-plans-to-cost-15-percent-more-soon); [TelecomTalk, 6 July 2026](https://telecomtalk.info/jio-airtel-vi-could-hike-price-of/1009430/)). Jefferies called the timing in January — a 15% hike around June 2026, two years after the last one, more than doubling sector revenue growth to roughly 16% in FY27 ([NDTV Profit, 8 January 2026](https://www.ndtvprofit.com/business/mobile-tariffs-to-go-up-by-15-in-june-2026-jio-ipo-to-boost-sector-valuation-jefferies)). The hike was not a response to events. It was on the calendar — and the fact that June came and went with the hike merely sliding to October–November, not disappearing, makes the pre-scripting point more strongly, not less.

The structure that makes this possible deserves stating plainly. India had 10–15 telecom operators before 2016; Jio's free-data launch that September triggered a price war that eliminated Aircel, Reliance Communications, Tata Teleservices, Telenor and MTS, forced the Vodafone–Idea merger, and left three private operators plus a fading BSNL by 2019 ([ICLE](https://www.icle.in/resource/telecom-consolidations-and-the-idea-of-essential-facilities-the-jio-case-revisited/)). TRAI's April 2026 subscription data puts the census at Jio 499 million, Airtel 481 million, Vi 199 million, BSNL 93 million ([tele.net.in, 3 June 2026](https://tele.net.in/trai-releases-its-telecom-subscription-report-for-april-2026/)). The regulator, meanwhile, practises tariff forbearance: it does not set prices, only disclosure rules — a doctrine designed for a crowded market, now applied to a three-player one.

## How does the entry-price ratchet actually work?

A headline tariff hike raises the price of every plan. A floor-raise does something subtler: it deletes the cheapest plan and promotes the next tier into the entry slot. Nothing is "hiked"; the bottom of the menu simply ceases to exist.

Airtel's staircase is the cleanest example. The entry floor stepped from ₹249 to ₹299 in August 2025, and now to ₹349. That is a 17% rise in the minimum cost of staying connected in twelve months, and a 40% rise over roughly two years — executed, in the latest step, without a single tariff-hike headline. The arithmetic does double duty. Every user at the old floor must either pay up to the new tier — converting the lowest-value customers into higher-ARPU customers — or leave, which raises ARPU anyway by shrinking the denominator. Meanwhile, at the top of the pyramid, 2G-to-5G migration and bundled subscriptions push spending the other way. Airtel added 21.1 million smartphone data users in a year, taking that base to 301.8 million.

The results are visible in the June 2026 quarter, before any of the coming hike has landed. Airtel's India mobile ARPU reached ₹264 and consolidated net profit rose 37% year on year to ₹8,167 crore ([The Economic Times, 4 August 2026](https://m.economictimes.com/markets/stocks/earnings/bharti-airtel-q1-results-net-profit-surges-37-to-rs-8167-crore-arpu-rises-to-rs-264/articleshow/132858553.cms)). Jio reported ARPU of ₹215.6, net profit of ₹7,764 crore, and a subscriber base past 533 million ([Moneycontrol, 17 July 2026](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/telecom/jio-platforms-q1-net-profit-up-9-2-arpu-at-rs-215-6-13977036.html)). Across FY26, the two leaders earned a combined ~₹64,000 crore in net profit — Airtel ₹33,823 crore, Jio ₹30,053 crore — a figure former government secretary E.A.S. Sarma put at the centre of his July letter to the DoT opposing the hikes ([Countercurrents, 5 July 2026](https://countercurrents.org/2026/07/airtel-jios-proposed-tariff-increases-are-unjust/)).

Why can three companies move in lockstep without consequence? Because their customers have nowhere to go. When all three raised prices within days of each other in July 2024, port-outs were simply absorbed within the oligopoly. Parallel pricing in a transparent, concentrated market is legal, predictable, and — this is the part that matters — indistinguishable from coordination from the consumer's side of the bill.

## Why is this hike different from the last three?

The 2019 and 2021 hikes were triage: the industry was loss-making after the price war, and ARPU had fallen below ₹100. The 2024 hike was repair. The 2026 hike is something else — discretionary. The two leaders are earning record profits, Jio turned free-cash-flow positive in FY25 as capex fell, and Goldman Sachs expects Airtel's India return on capital employed to exceed 20% by FY27 ([ET Telecom, May 2025](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/telecom/telecom-news/jio-airtel-to-grow-on-user-gains-tariff-hike-analysts/articleshow/121275617.cms)).

The capex justification is also wearing thin. The 2022 5G spectrum auction cost the industry a record ₹1.5 lakh crore — Jio bid ₹88,078 crore, Airtel ₹43,084 crore ([Hindustan Times](https://www.hindustantimes.com/business/indias-telecom-spectrum-auction-ends-early-on-day-two-only-12-of-spectrum-sold-101719405932693.html)). But the coverage build is essentially complete: 5.08 lakh base stations, 99.9% of districts covered, over 400 million 5G subscribers by late 2025 ([Legacy IAS](https://www.legacyias.com/5g-technology-upsc-notes/)). The marginal rupee of new tariff in 2026 increasingly funds returns rather than new coverage — even allowing for the deferred spectrum installments from that auction, which are still construction-era bills the operators are paying off. "Tariff repair" described the 2019–2024 phase honestly; at ₹215–264 ARPU with record profits, the honest phrase is margin expansion.

The political surface area has changed too. Telecom is a CPI item — the July 2024 hike added roughly 0.2 percentage points to FY25 core inflation ([ET, 3 July 2024](https://m.economictimes.com/news/economy/indicators/telcos-tariff-hikes-to-push-up-core-inflation-by-0-2-pc-in-fy25-report/articleshow/111465152.cms)) — and it is landing into a June 2026 CPI print of 4.38% ([MoSPI](https://www.mospi.gov.in/themes/product/9-consumer-price-index-cpi)). And this time the regulator has a live counter-move on the table. TRAI's draft Thirteenth Amendment would force operators to offer a voice-and-SMS-only voucher for every validity period, priced proportionately below the bundled plans ([MediaNama, 16 June 2026](https://www.medianama.com/2026/06/223-jio-airtel-vi-trai-voice-sms-only-plan/)). All three private operators opposed it at the open house on 15 June 2026 — as they did when TRAI first mandated such a voucher in 2024, after which they complied minimally, offering it only at 84-day and 365-day validities without proportionate price cuts. That minimal compliance is exactly why the 2026 amendment widens the mandate.

## Is this capex recovery or rent extraction?

There are at least four honest readings of this cycle, and they are not mutually exclusive.

**The operators' case: legitimate recovery in a capital-intensive industry.** Indian tariffs remain among the world's lowest in absolute terms, as Airtel's Gopal Vittal reminds anyone who will listen; ₹1.5 lakh crore of spectrum and years of rollout capex must earn a return; returns on capital were below the cost of capital for most of 2016–2022. The weakness in this reading is timing. It describes 2019 perfectly. In 2026, with the build complete and profits at records, the recovery case has become a margin case wearing recovery's clothes.

**The consumer case: oligopoly rent extraction under forbearance.** Synchronised pricing twice in two years; competition conducted by deleting cheap plans rather than improving service; united opposition to a voice-only voucher that would end the cross-subsidy. The strongest datum here is distributional: consumer groups calculate that entry-level packs work out to ₹94–99 per effective GB, many times the per-GB rate on premium plans — the poorest users pay the highest unit price for data in the system ([MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/06/223-jio-airtel-vi-trai-voice-sms-only-plan/)). Per-GB arithmetic always flatters big bundles, so some gap is structural; a gap of this size is not. Roughly 150 million Indians on basic and feature phones sit at that end of the menu. The counterargument is that forbearance is a policy choice, not capture: the state has instruments — TRAI's consumer-protection powers, BSNL as a price anchor — and has chosen not to use them, which is a contestable trade but not an illegitimate one.

**The fiscal reading: recapitalisation by stealth.** Follow Vodafone Idea. Vi's ARPU is ₹190, the lowest of the three; in Q3 FY26 its cash EBITDA of ₹2,358 crore covered less than half its ₹5,632 crore of finance costs ([Bulletinloop](https://www.bulletinloop.com/news/vodafone-idea-vi-stock-agr-relief-5g-2026-long-term-outlook)). Its AGR dues have been reassessed to ₹64,046 crore and frozen until 2041, making the government its largest creditor ([Multibagg.ai](https://www.multibagg.ai/market-pulse/articles/vodafone-idea-agr-dues-freeze-cmoqmwpt078ubp50jt6i9k5iw)). JM Financial estimates Vi needs ARPU of ~₹340 by FY29 to meet cash outflows from internal accruals — a path that assumes tariff hikes land on schedule. Vi's pending ₹25,000 crore bank raise is conditioned on those hikes. Every turn of the ratchet therefore improves the recoverable value of public claims. The flaw in this reading is that it implies a single hand where the state has several, pulling in different directions.

**The benign reading: this is cyclical, not structural.** Hikes came in 2019, 2021, 2024 — a two-year cadence that will meet demand elasticity eventually. The problem is that a ratchet with pauses looks cyclical until you plot the entry price over a decade. None of the previous increases was ever given back, and elasticity requires an exit door. Connectivity is now the substrate for UPI, Aadhaar-linked services and gig work; the share of users who can actually leave the system is negligible.

## Has Indian telecom been here before?

Twice in this industry, once in a neighbouring one.

The 2019–2021 repair cycle established the template: lockstep increases, minimal subscriber loss, ARPU roughly doubling off its trough. It is useful now mainly for one demonstration — the floor only moves one way. But the politics then and now are inverted. A loss-making industry raising prices to survive is defensible as triage. A record-profit duopoly-plus-one raising prices on 150 million feature-phone users must be defended as a choice, and choices have owners.

The July 2024 hike calibrates the macro stakes: an 11–25% increase across operators, negligible churn, ~0.2 percentage points on core inflation. Tenths of a point, not points. The binding constraint on telecom pricing has never been commercial. It is political.

The structural memory worth keeping is Indian aviation. Between 2012 and 2023 the airlines consolidated from a fragmented field into an IndiGo–Air India duopoly. Fares firmed, complaints rose, and the affordability argument migrated from the market to the regulator and the courts — where it lives today. Telecom has two features aviation lacked: a state-owned residual anchor in BSNL, and a government that sits inside the capital structure of the weakest player. Whether those features change the end-state or merely delay it is the open part of the analogy. It is [another Indian sector where state policy shapes the market structure](final/signal-ola-electric-pli-ev-battery-carousel-standalone), though the mechanism there is subsidy rather than pricing.

## What does a 15% hike touch beyond the phone bill?

Start with inflation. A 12–15% hike landing in late 2026 feeds the CPI's communication basket directly and services-inflation expectations indirectly. Against a 4.38% print, the 2024 template says the direct effect is small; the complication is that it arrives [while the RBI is weighing an easing path](final/signal-central-bank-divergence-rbi), and telecom is one of the few administered-feeling prices in the basket.

Then the fisc. The government holds ₹64,046 crore of reassessed AGR claims on Vi, deferred-spectrum receivables from all three operators, and equity in Vi itself. Telecom pricing is, in effect, a line item in India's fiscal arithmetic — an unusual position for a consumer price. If the hike lands, Vi's bank raise gets easier to place and bank exposure to the sector de-risks further. If it stalls on political pushback, the funding gap reopens and the contingent liability returns to the sovereign.

Finally, the layer above. Every consumer-internet business in India — payments, commerce, gig work, streaming — runs on the connectivity layer whose entry price is being ratcheted. A 15% rise in the access cost of the user base is a modest headwind to penetration-led growth stories, and it falls hardest on exactly the next hundred million users those stories are made of. If the hike lands on the ₹349 floor rather than above it, the floor becomes roughly ₹400 — a 60% rise in the cost of basic connectivity in two years, pricing out the marginal rural user the digital-public-infrastructure stack was built to serve. The Jio IPO, expected to be absorbed by [the domestic bid](final/signal-india-domestic-flow-market-blog), will be priced off post-hike ARPU.

## What happens next — and who can stop the ratchet?

The ratchet will keep turning until something with power objects, and the inventory of objects is short. TRAI's voice-only amendment is one — its final definition of "proportionately lower" is where the fight over the 150 million non-data users will be won or lost. BSNL's 93 million subscribers make it a theoretical price anchor, though the anchor itself is slipping: BSNL lost over a lakh subscribers in February 2026 alone while Airtel added 4.86 million, and anchors can drag as well as hold. The market itself will not object; that is what a three-player structure means.

Which leaves the government, sitting in four chairs at once: affordability guardian through DoT and TRAI, largest creditor to the weakest operator, shareholder in that operator, and owner of the public alternative. Each turn of the ratchet serves three of those roles and betrays the fourth. The 2019 hikes were existential, so the conflict was dormant. The 2026 hike is discretionary, so it is not.

So the question to carry forward is not whether tariffs rise 12% or 15% by Diwali. It is this: at what floor price does the phone bill of 150 million feature-phone users stop being a regulatory matter and become an electoral one — and when it does, which of the state's four chairs answers? *See [the four-chair conflict in one frame](final/signal-telecom-tariff-hikes-sector-carousel-standalone).*
