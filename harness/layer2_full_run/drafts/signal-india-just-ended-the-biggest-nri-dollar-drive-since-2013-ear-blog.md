**India Just Ended the Biggest NRI Dollar Drive Since 2013 — Early**

## The RBI closed its $56.8 billion dollar-collection window a month ahead of schedule, nine days after promising it wouldn't — and the interesting question is which problem that solved.

Imagine a neighbourhood that starts collecting rainwater during a drought. The tank fills so fast that the organisers announce, mid-monsoon, that collection ends early. Everyone cheers: the drought must be over. But there is a second reading. The organisers also own the pipes, and every litre collected flows through plumbing that has to carry it back out in three to five years. Maybe the tank is full. Maybe the pipes are the constraint. On August 14, the Reserve Bank of India did the central-banking version of this. It ended its special NRI dollar deposit drive a month early, citing success. The success is real. So is the plumbing.

## Context: a window that closed before it was supposed to

On June 8, 2026, the RBI opened a special swap facility tied to FCNR(B) deposits — the dollar accounts non-resident Indians hold at Indian banks. Banks could raise eligible three-to-five-year deposits until September 30, and swap the proceeds with the RBI until October 16.

On August 14, both dates moved forward. Mobilisation now ends August 31; swapping ends September 11. The stated reason: total inflows through the scheme had crossed $56.8 billion by August 13, of which $52.3 billion came through FCNR(B) deposits alone, per data reported by authorised dealer banks (CNBC-TV18, August 14).

The timing carries its own information. On August 5, at the post-policy press briefing, Governor Sanjay Malhotra had said there were no plans to close the window prematurely. Nine days later, the window was closed prematurely. Operational notices are not speeches, but they communicate all the same.

## Mechanism: the RBI became the hedge

A bank that takes dollar deposits and deploys rupees in India has a currency mismatch. Normally it hedges that mismatch in the forward market, and the annualised cost of the hedge caps the deposit rate it can offer.

The special facility removed that cap. A bank raising an eligible FCNR(B) deposit could swap the dollars with the RBI: dollars to the central bank today, rupees to the bank, and at maturity the same trade in reverse at the same exchange rate. Tenor-matched, at par. The RBI absorbed the entire hedging cost. That is why banks could offer up to 7.8% on dollar deposits — a rate that hedged market economics would never have supported (Moneycontrol, August 17).

Three consequences follow, and they are the whole story.

First, the RBI is now the hedge leg. Every swapped dollar is a forward liability: in 2029–2031, the RBI must deliver those dollars back to banks at today's exchange rate, wherever USD/INR then trades.

Second, every swapped dollar injects rupees. The rupee leg lands as banking-system liquidity — one reason average surplus liquidity sits near Rs 3 lakh crore, and deposit growth touched 15.4% for the fortnight ended July 15.

Third, the subsidy is invisible until it isn't. Unlike spot intervention, the swap costs nothing observable today. Its cost is the forward premium forgone, plus a concentrated cluster of future dollar-delivery obligations.

One companion notice completes the picture. On August 7, the RBI excluded loans made against these fresh FCNR(B) and NRE deposits from banks' Adjusted Net Bank Credit for priority-sector calculations (Moneycontrol, August 7). Credit funded by NRI dollars was quietly ring-fenced from the regulated lending baseline — an admission, in plumbing language, that this credit needed segregating.

## Signal: what is unusual

Three deviations stand out against history and expectation.

**Scale.** The 2013 episode — the direct template — raised roughly $26 billion through FCNR(B) deposits over three months. The 2026 scheme raised $52.3 billion in ten weeks.

**Velocity.** The first $20 billion took 38 days to arrive. The next $20 billion took 14. In the 13 days to August 13 alone, $16 billion came in (NDTV Profit, August 15). The scheme did not approach its target; it accelerated through it.

**The rupee didn't move.** On June 5, USD/INR closed at 95.39. On August 14, it closed at 95.45 — after a mid-July recovery to 94.30 faded. More than $50 billion of subsidised inflows bought reserves, not currency relief.

## Interpretation spectrum: two honest readings

**Reading one: oversubscription success.** SBI Research called the truncation "a prudent decision considering the optimal corpus achievement." Karur Vysya Bank's treasury head read it as "calibration rather than a change in policy direction." The evidence cooperates: inflows ran at double the 2013 precedent, $31.2 billion of reserves had already been recouped by August 7, and the marginal subsidised dollar was adding more liquidity than buffer. A central bank that has filled the tank stops collecting.

What weakens this reading is the sequence. On August 5, with $50 billion-plus already visible, the Governor publicly ruled out early closure. Success was knowable nine days earlier. If the target was simply met, the reversal was oddly timed.

**Reading two: carry discomfort.** Treasury heads and a former central banker quoted by Moneycontrol point at cost: "Other than the cost implication, I don't see any major compelling reason." The forwards market, one noted, was "not moving in a way that will facilitate economic hedging by the RBI." Supporting evidence: the rupee gained nothing over the scheme's life, meaning the subsidy was being paid without currency relief; the RBI was reportedly selling dollars in spot even while committing to deliver dollars forward; and its net short forward book was already around $106.6 billion at end-May, on market-reported figures.

What weakens this reading: the ECB and overseas-borrowing swap window was left open until December 31. A central bank panicking about carry does not close one leg and leave the other running.

Both readings may be partially true — success made closure affordable; cost made it attractive. What is known: the dates, the flows, the PSL notice, and the nine-day reversal. What is unknown: the RBI's internal target, the swap book's maturity split, and the realised carry. What is unknowable: where USD/INR trades when the swaps come due.

## Historical memory: 2013, used carefully

In September 2013, mid-taper-tantrum, the RBI offered FCNR(B) swaps at a concessional 3.5% — about three percentage points below market — and raised roughly $34 billion across two windows. The realised cost ended up lower than feared because the rupee stabilised.

Two differences matter more than the similarity. The 2013 window charged banks a concessional rate; the 2026 window absorbs the full hedging cost at par. And 2013's window ran to its announced end. It was never closed early. As SPJIMR's Ananth Narayan later documented, much of 2013's "NRI money" was overseas bank money lent to NRIs at up to 19x leverage, and India effectively paid around 5% all-in for three-year dollars — a price a sovereign bond would have made embarrassingly visible. The same leveraged structures reportedly reappeared in 2026, with exposures of 9x to 19x. History here offers perspective, not prediction: it teaches that headline mobilisation overstates true diaspora money, and that the state's real borrowing cost hides in the subsidy.

## Implications: what this affects, and what it does not

This affects how to read India's reserve adequacy. Headline reserves of $692.9 billion increasingly include swap-funded dollars that must be returned in three to five years; metrics that net out forward liabilities will tell a different story from headline foreign currency assets.

It affects the bond market's plumbing. SBI Research estimates the drive channels Rs 8–9 lakh crore into the banking system, with the 3–7-year government bond segment the clearest beneficiary — maturity-matched to the deposits. And it affects forward premia: as the RBI exits as the subsidised hedge leg, the 3–5-year bucket loses a distorting presence.

It does not change the rupee's drivers — oil, portfolio flows, and the dollar's global cycle. It is not a signal about deposit safety; FCNR(B) deposits continue as a product. And it is not, in itself, a reason for any portfolio decision. It is a disclosure about the state's own balance sheet, delivered in operational language.

## Open question

In 2029–2031, the swaps unwind: banks hand back rupees, the RBI hands back dollars, at August 2026 exchange rates. Whether that unwind is a footnote or an event depends on a variable no one controls — where USD/INR stands when the season arrives. The RBI has spent ten weeks building a reserve buffer and a matching liability, and has now decided, earlier than planned, that it has enough of both. The question worth carrying forward is simpler than the plumbing: what does the RBI's forward book look like if the rupee retests its record low before the swaps even season?
