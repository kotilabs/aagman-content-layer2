## Research Artifact — FCNR(B) Early Window Close (August 2026)

**Signal ID:** india-just-ended-the-biggest-nri-dollar-drive-since-2013-ear
**Compiled:** 2026-08-18

---

### 1. Signal restatement

On August 14, 2026, the Reserve Bank of India shortened the mobilisation window of its special FCNR(B) USD-INR swap facility by one month: banks may now raise eligible deposits only until August 31 (previously September 30), and may swap those dollars with the RBI only until September 11 (previously October 16). The stated reason is the strength of the response — total forex inflows through the scheme crossed $56.8 billion by August 13, of which $52.3 billion came through FCNR(B) deposits. The timing is striking because nine days earlier, on August 5, Governor Sanjay Malhotra had publicly ruled out premature closure. The signal matters because it is a policy statement delivered through an operational notice: either the RBI overshot an internal target faster than expected, or the carrying cost and forward-book exposure of the swap book became uncomfortable. The answer bears directly on how observers should read India's reserve adequacy and the state of the RBI's forward book into year-end.

### 4. Mechanism

An FCNR(B) deposit is a foreign-currency term deposit an NRI places with an Indian bank. Normally, a bank that takes dollars and deploys rupees in India must hedge the currency mismatch in the forward market — and the forward premium (the annualised cost of that hedge) caps the deposit rate the bank can profitably offer. Under the June 8 special facility, an authorised-dealer bank that raises a fresh 3–5-year FCNR(B) deposit can swap the dollars with the RBI: dollars go to the RBI today, rupees come to the bank, and at deposit maturity the bank returns rupees and takes its dollars back at the same exchange rate. Tenor-matched and at par, the RBI absorbs the entire forward-hedging cost. That is what let banks offer 7%+ on dollar deposits when hedged economics would otherwise have capped them far lower.

Three consequences follow, and they are the whole editorial story:

1. **The RBI became the hedge leg.** Every swapped dollar is a forward liability on the RBI's balance sheet: in 3–5 years it must deliver those dollars back to banks regardless of where USD/INR trades. Tens of billions of such swaps now mature in a cluster in 2029–2031.
2. **Every swapped dollar injects rupees.** The rupee leg lands as banking-system liquidity — the reason average surplus liquidity sits near Rs 3 lakh crore and deposit growth hit 15.4%. If the RBI doesn't want that liquidity, it must sterilise it through its normal tools, which has its own cost.
3. **The subsidy is off-balance-sheet until it isn't.** Unlike a spot intervention, the swap costs nothing visible today. The cost is the carry (forward premium forgone) plus the concentration of future dollar delivery obligations. Whether that carry "became uncomfortable" is exactly what the early closure is being read through.

The August 7 PSL companion notice fits the same plumbing logic: loans extended in India against these fresh FCNR(B)/NRE deposits are excluded from Adjusted Net Bank Credit for priority-sector computation. NRI-dollar-funded rupee credit was being generated outside the normal PSL accounting — the RBI ring-fenced it so that deposit-driven credit expansion would not contaminate the priority-sector ratios banks must meet. It is a quiet admission that the credit funded by this drive needed segregating from the regulated lending baseline.

### 5. Competing interpretations

**Interpretation A — Oversubscription success ("job done, close the tap").**
- Who: SBI Research ("prudent decision... optimal corpus achievement"), Karur Vysya's Ramachandra Reddy ("calibration rather than course correction"), the Finnovate explainer's editorial line.
- Evidence for: velocity of inflows ($16bn in 13 days); FCNR(B) alone at ~2x the entire 2013 haul; $31.2bn of reserves already recouped; SBI's own pre-closure estimate of $70bn suggests the RBI's internal target — whatever it was — was comfortably within reach.
- Evidence against: the Governor ruled out early closure on August 5, when $50bn+ was already visible. If the target was simply met, the reversal within nine days is oddly sequenced.
- Why it might be wrong: success alone doesn't explain the timing; the marginal dollar's benefit doesn't fall off a cliff between August 5 and August 14.

**Interpretation B — Carry-cost and forward-book discomfort ("the hedge leg got expensive").**
- Who: unnamed treasury heads and a former central banker quoted by Moneycontrol ("other than the cost implication, I don't see any major compelling reason"; forwards market "not moving in a way that will facilitate economic hedging").
- Evidence for: the rupee went nowhere despite $50bn+ of inflows (95.39 → 95.45), meaning the RBI was paying the swap subsidy without receiving currency relief; reported spot dollar selling alongside a ~$106.6bn net short forward book implies the RBI was already heavily committed on the sell-dollars-forward side; leverage structures (9x–19x) were inflating the quality-adjusted value of the inflows.
- Evidence against: the ECB/OFCB swap window was left open until December 31 — if carry cost were the dominant concern, closing the deposit leg while leaving the borrowing leg open is a selective response.
- Why it might be wrong: "carry discomfort" is inference from market colour, not any RBI statement; the RBI's public line is only about inflow strength.

**Interpretation C — Liability-quality control ("stop the leveraged money, keep the real money").**
- Who: implied by the August 7 PSL ring-fencing and by commentary on leveraged FCNR(B) structures (Jefferies-reported 17–27% leveraged returns; Finnovate's 9x–19x exposure range).
- Evidence for: the 2013 lesson — most of that "$26bn in NRI money" was overseas bank money lent to NRIs; the PSL exclusion a week before closure shows the RBI was already segregating this credit; closing early caps the growth of leverage-fuelled deposits while letting genuine deposits finish landing.
- Evidence against: no RBI statement links the closure to leverage; banks' FCNR(B) rates up to 7.8% attracted real diaspora money too.
- Why it might be wrong: if leverage were the core worry, regulatory action against SBLC-backed structures would be the cleaner tool than ending the whole swap window early.

**Interpretation D — Reserve-adequacy signalling ("we are no longer desperate for dollars").**
- Who: a read-through from market participants, not attributed commentary.
- Evidence for: reserves at $692.9bn by July 31 and rising; ending an emergency-style instrument early projects confidence in the external position — the opposite message of launching it.
- Evidence against: the rupee was at record lows in May and still near 95.5; a confident central bank could equally let the window run to schedule and bank the extra dollars.
- Why it might be wrong: signalling confidence by reversing a nine-day-old public assurance costs credibility — an odd way to send a reassuring message.

### 6. Cross-asset implications

- **USD/INR spot and forwards:** The swap window's closure removes the marginal subsidised buyer of NRI dollars, but the flow itself continues. The bigger effect is on forward premia: the RBI stepping back as the subsidised hedge leg removes a distorting presence in the 3–5y tenor; premia in that bucket should re-price toward market clearing as the window closes. If USD/INR retests record levels before the swaps season matures, the RBI carries the mark on both the legacy forward book and the new swap book.
- **Indian government bonds:** SBI Research projects the drive channels Rs 8–9 lakh crore into the banking system and lowers counterfactual G-sec yields, with the 3–7y segment the clearest beneficiary (maturity-matched to the deposit tenors), 7–10y next; the 15–50y end still carries oil/inflation/fiscal-supply premium. Transmission: swapped rupees → bank balance sheets → demand for duration at matching maturities.
- **Bank balance sheets and credit:** Deposit growth at 15.4% (fortnight to July 15) against credit growth at 19.3%; the PSL exclusion means this incremental credit doesn't count toward priority-sector targets, so banks' true PSL compliance ratios are unaffected by the NRI-funded book — a cleaner read of underlying credit quality but a regulatory carve-out worth watching.
- **FX reserves composition:** Headline reserves rose ($692.9bn by July 31), but composition matters: an increasing share is swap-funded — dollars that must be returned in 3–5 years. Reserve-adequacy metrics that net out forward liabilities will look different from headline FCA.
- **EM FX broadly:** A large EM central bank subsidising reserve accumulation and then ending the subsidy early is a data point on how far dollar strength has pushed EM policy into liability management; watch for copycat or contrast cases in other deficit EMs.
- **Derivatives:** The 2029–2031 maturity cluster creates a known future dollar-demand event; onshore forward curves may begin pricing that seasonality well before maturity.

### 7. Historical analogues or structural memory

**Analogue 1 — The 2013 Rajan-era FCNR(B) swap window (the direct template).**
- Episode: September 2013, post-taper-tantrum, with India in the "fragile five." Two concessional swap windows: FCNR(B) deposits of 3+ years swapped at 3.5% p.a. (~3.0% below market); bank foreign-currency borrowings at 1% below market. Together they raised ~$34bn (~$26bn via FCNR(B)), helping stabilise the rupee and rebuild reserves.
- Key similarity: same instrument, same purpose — the central bank absorbs hedging cost to convert NRI/bank dollar-raising into reserve accumulation during currency stress. Both episodes paired the facility with deposit-regulation sweeteners (2013: rate deregulation; 2026: ceiling removal plus CRR/SLR exemption).
- Key differences: (a) 2026's swap is at par/tenor-matched — the RBI absorbs the full hedging cost, versus a fixed 3.5% concessional rate in 2013; (b) scale — $52.3bn of FCNR(B) in ten weeks versus ~$26bn over three months; (c) macro backdrop — 2013 had a wide current-account deficit, rate hikes and liquidity tightening; 2026 has record-low prints driven by an oil shock and FPI outflows but a far larger reserve base; (d) 2013's window ran to its announced November end — it was not closed early.
- Why useful for perspective, not prediction: 2013 shows the scheme's realised cost ended up lower than feared because USD/INR stabilised after success — but that outcome depended on the currency turning. It frames the question ("what does the RBI's swap book look like if the rupee doesn't turn?") without answering it.

**Analogue 2 — The 2013 leverage unwind lesson (the cautionary sub-plot).**
- Episode: much of the 2013 money was overseas bank money lent to NRIs at up to 19x leverage; India effectively paid ~5.00% all-in for 3-year dollars (~4.35% over USTs) — what Narayan calls a high, hidden price that a sovereign bond at that yield would have made embarrassingly public.
- Key similarity: 2026 shows the same structure re-emerging — SBLC-backed leveraged placements, reported 9x–19x exposures, 17–27% estimated leveraged returns.
- Key difference: the 2026 RBI appears to have anticipated the critique — the PSL exclusion and the early closure both read as ring-fencing the leveraged segment rather than celebrating headline inflows.
- Why useful: it teaches that headline mobilisation overstates true diaspora money and understates the state's effective borrowing cost — directly relevant to judging whether $56.8bn is the triumph the headline implies.

### 8. What is known, unknown, and unknowable

**Known:** the revised dates (mobilise by Aug 31, swap by Sep 11); the flow data ($56.85bn total, $52.3bn FCNR(B), velocity profile); that the Governor ruled out early closure on Aug 5; the Aug 7 PSL exclusion and its terms; that reserves were rebuilt by $31.2bn as of Aug 7; that the rupee gained nothing over the scheme's life; that the ECB/OFCB swap leg stays open to Dec 31; the 2013 precedent's terms, size, and effective cost.

**Unknown:** the RBI's internal mobilisation target; the exact size and maturity distribution of the new swap book (3y vs 5y split); the realised carry cost the RBI is absorbing; how much of the $52.3bn is genuinely unleveraged diaspora money versus financed structures; whether the closure decision was driven primarily by success, by cost, or by liability quality; the precise end-May forward-book figure ($106.6bn is market-reported, not from an RBI weekly statistical release checked here).

**Unknowable:** where USD/INR trades when the 2029–2031 swap maturities come due; whether the forward book behaves benignly if the rupee retests record lows before the swaps season; how much of the inflow would have arrived without the subsidy (the counterfactual is unobservable); whether the early closure will be remembered as calibration or as the first visible strain in the reserve-accumulation strategy.

---

### 9. Open questions

1. What share of the $52.3bn FCNR(B) mobilisation is leveraged (SBLC-backed) money versus outright diaspora deposits — and will any disclosure or data release ever let outsiders estimate it?
2. What is the maturity distribution of the swap book, and how large is the 2029–2031 dollar-delivery cluster relative to projected reserves then?
3. Do onshore 3–5y forward premia re-price materially now that the RBI is exiting as the subsidised hedge leg — and does that raise hedging costs for ordinary corporates?
4. Does the ECB/OFCB window (open to December 31) pull in the rumoured $15bn, and does that change the interpretation of why the deposit leg specifically was closed?
5. If USD/INR retests 96 before year-end, does the RBI re-open a subsidised inflow channel — and what would a second reversal do to the signalling value of operational notices?
