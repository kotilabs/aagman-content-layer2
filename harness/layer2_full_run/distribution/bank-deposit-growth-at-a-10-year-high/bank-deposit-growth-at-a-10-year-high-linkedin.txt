India's bank deposits grew 15.4% year-on-year as of July 31, the fastest pace in more than a decade. Two weeks earlier, the number was 12.7%.

No organic shift in how a country saves moves a deposit base above Rs 260 trillion by 270 basis points in a single fortnight. Something else did.

Between April 1 and June 15, Indian banks actually lost Rs 3.87 trillion in deposits. Then, over the three fortnights to July 31, the system added Rs 11 trillion, taking total deposits to a record Rs 269.4 trillion. Traders quoted by Reuters attribute the jump largely to one piece of RBI plumbing: a special dollar-swap window for FCNR(B) deposits, opened June 8, that let banks convert NRI dollars into rupees at a fixed rate. It pulled in $52.3 billion before the RBI closed it early, and $36.7 billion of that arrived in exactly the weeks the growth print jumped.

That timing is what separates this from a normal deposit cycle: the easing came from a policy lever, while household deposit behaviour is not separately visible in the data. Even so, credit is still growing faster than deposits, 19.3% against 15.4%, so the funding pressure that squeezed bank margins through FY25 and FY26 has eased without closing. Banks with deep NRI franchises receive the windfall first; everyone else still bids for household savings.

A measurement caveat is worth holding as well: the RBI changed its fortnight reporting convention in December 2025, and this data is provisional.

The open question is the next print. The window takes its last deposits on August 31. Does growth hold near 15%, or mean-revert toward 12-13%?
