Brent is at $89. In rupee terms, India is paying for $112.

On August 17, Brent settled at $89 a barrel while the rupee closed at 95.61 per dollar, about 1% off its record low. One barrel now costs India ₹8,509. In May 2022, $112 oil at ₹77.8 cost ₹8,728 a barrel. The gap between a $112 shock and an $89 shock is 3%. The currency supplied the rest.

The climb since January is sharper still: the same barrel cost about ₹5,670 then, so the rupee cost of crude is up roughly 50% in seven months. At today's levels the two dials are nearly equal in power. Each $1 rise in Brent adds about ₹0.60 a litre to crude cost; each ₹1 slide in the rupee adds about ₹0.56.

Retail prices have not followed. Delhi petrol has been frozen at ₹102.12 since the ₹3 hike of May 15, the first revision since April 2022. The freeze converts a commodity shock into a margin shock: the arithmetic puts OMC breakeven near Brent $92-99 at this exchange rate, which means at $89 the cushion exists but is thin. April and May showed what lies past that line, when combined OMC under-recoveries ran near ₹30,000 crore a month.

Who bears the cost if Brent holds here: OMC shareholders first, then the fisc through LPG under-recoveries already running ₹23,700 crore above the compensation budgeted, and eventually the CPI. June's print caught the May hike within a month. If the pump stays frozen, the August print due September 14 will look calm precisely because the pressure has been rerouted.

The open question: what triggers the next retail revision, a Brent level, a rupee level, or an OMC loss threshold?
