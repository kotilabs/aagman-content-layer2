On August 14, the RBI closed its special FCNR(B) dollar window a month ahead of schedule. Nine days earlier, the Governor had publicly ruled out doing exactly that.

What happened: the scheme, opened June 8, let banks swap NRI dollar deposits with the RBI at par, tenor-matched. The central bank absorbed the entire hedging cost, which is how banks could offer up to 7.8% on dollar deposits. Money arrived at a pace nobody modeled. The first $20 billion took 38 days, the next $20 billion took 14, and $16 billion came in the final 13 days. Total inflows crossed $56.8 billion by August 13; FCNR(B) deposits alone hit $52.3 billion, double the entire 2013 haul.

The mechanism cuts both ways. Every swapped dollar is a forward liability: between 2029 and 2031, the RBI must return those dollars at today's exchange rate. And every swapped dollar injects rupees, one reason surplus liquidity sits near Rs 3 lakh crore and SBI Research projects Rs 8-9 lakh crore eventually flowing into the banking system, with 3-7 year government bonds the clearest beneficiary.

Why this instance differs from 2013: that window charged banks a concessional 3.5% and ran to its announced end. This one absorbed the full hedging cost, and was closed early.

Who bears the cost is the open question. The rupee moved from 95.39 to 95.45 over the scheme's life; the subsidy was paid without currency relief. The RBI's net short forward book was reportedly $106.6 billion at end-May. Oversubscription success and carry discomfort both fit the facts. The nine-day reversal fits neither cleanly.
