**Slide 1 (cover)**

China is squeezing Taiwan's chip chain without banning anything. Customs just got slower.

Germanium and quartz shipments to Taiwan have faced clearance delays since 2025.
The market's first reaction claimed more than the evidence shows.

**Slide 2**

On August 20, Nikkei Asia reported that Taiwanese optical manufacturers have faced lengthy customs checks on germanium and quartz from Chinese suppliers since last year. Some suppliers were summoned and questioned about their customers. One executive said orders were already lost to longer lead times. No ban was announced. Clearance simply started taking longer.

**Slide 3**

The legal base is older. Beijing has licensed every gallium and germanium export since August 2023, and since December 2024 generally denies licenses for dual-use shipments to the US. That leaves two dials: formal denial, and clearance speed. The second is deniable, reversible, and hard to challenge.

**Slide 4**

The verified impact: optical makers report industrywide lead-time extensions; one chip equipment executive says quartz shortages have stretched his company's lead time by months; aerospace suppliers report trouble with Chinese magnets. What no source confirms is a wafer fab cutting output.

**Slide 5**

The prices tell you where the squeeze lives. Chinese domestic germanium fell 9.2 percent in August to $3,104 a kilo. American warehouse germanium held at $6,350. The Western premium widened to 104.6 percent. The scarcity is in physical availability outside China, not in the headline price.

**Slide 6**

Supply cannot respond. Germanium is a byproduct of zinc smelting, about 130 to 140 tonnes a year worldwide, 80 percent of it in China. US shipments through September 2025 were already down nearly 80 percent from 2023, per USGS figures cited by Metal Tech News. Taiwan's domestic pilot line is three years away.

**Slide 7**

The market's first draft overshot. A trading desk note on August 23 turned the Nikkei report into "germanium is hitting Taiwan fab output" and a short case against TSMC, with positioning disclosed. The evidence supports delays for optics, equipment, and magnets. It does not support stalled wafer lines.

**Slide 8**

The chokepoint moved from prices to lead times. Nobody has yet shown it moving from lead times to lost wafer output.

**Slide 9**

In 2010, China informally held rare-earth shipments to Japan through customs during the Senkaku dispute, never announcing a ban. Markets spooked, Japan diversified, and the WTO later ruled against China's quotas. Weaponized supply worked once. This version is narrower and more careful.

**Slide 10**

The answer prints in documents. Taiwan's Q3 import data shows whether delays became a volume drop. September supplier commentary either includes allocation language or not. Any fab disclosing materials-inventory coverage settles the output claim in one line. Until then: the pressure is real, the targeting deliberate, and the biggest claim the one without evidence.
