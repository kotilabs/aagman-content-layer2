**Slide 1 (cover)**

America's debt just crossed $40 trillion, and the number that actually matters is not the debt. It is the bill.

US debt held by the public is at 99.4 percent of GDP, touching the 100 marker.
The interest line underneath has already crossed a different threshold.

**Slide 2**

On August 24, 2026, the Treasury's own daily ledger showed total public debt outstanding of $40.04 trillion, up roughly $970 billion since January 1, a pace above $4 billion a day. Debt held by the public, the stock the market actually absorbs, stood at $32.27 trillion. Against second quarter GDP of $32.48 trillion, that is 99.4 percent. The 100 percent milestone being celebrated and feared this week is, on the official arithmetic, a few weeks early.

**Slide 3**

The milestone is the least interesting number in the release. Net interest outlays reached a $1,247 billion annual rate in the second quarter, a series record. That is 16.1 percent of all federal outlays, meaning one dollar in six the government spends. It is 38.2 percent of federal receipts, meaning nearly 40 cents of every revenue dollar. In fiscal 2025 it was 3.15 percent of GDP, a three-decade high.

**Slide 4**

For calibration: net interest at $1,247 billion now exceeds the entire national defense budget, which the second quarter put at a $1,198 billion annual rate. The last time the interest share of GDP sat at this level was the early 1990s, and it helped force the deficit-reduction packages of 1990 and 1993. This time the interest share is the same, but the deficit behind it is 5.8 percent of GDP with no recession to justify it.

**Slide 5**

The mechanism is a conveyor belt, not a cliff. Paper issued at 2020 and 2021 coupons keeps maturing and getting refinanced at the Treasury's current average rates: 3.758 percent on bills, 3.309 percent on notes, 3.442 percent on bonds as of July. Every roll replaces cheap paper with current-rate paper. That is why the interest line kept climbing through 2023 to 2026 even as deficits narrowed from the pandemic peak. The convergence still has years to run.

**Slide 6**

What keeps the ratio arithmetic rather than exponential: the average rate on the stock, around 3.4 percent, sits below nominal GDP growth near 4 percent, and the world still buys Treasuries for reasons beyond yield, from reserves to collateral. What keeps it honest: a primary deficit this wide means the stabilizer only works while creditors believe the gap is managed. Solvency is not the question. The political economy of the interest line is.

**Slide 7**

The same feed that flagged the debt milestone tied it to consumer collapse, citing credit card delinquencies at 12.9 percent. That figure could not be verified against the NY Fed report. The verified household data points the other way: debt service at 11.16 percent of disposable income, stable, and bank charge-off rates on credit cards down from 4.19 percent in early 2025 to 3.82 percent. If there is stress, it is distributional, not aggregate.

**Slide 8**

The debt is a stock, the deficit is a flow, and the interest is the bill. Only the bill touches the budget, and the bill is at a record.

**Slide 9**

History offers two readings, both true. In 1946, public debt peaked at about 106 percent of GDP and fell to 23 percent by 1974, ground down by nominal growth and captive creditors under rate caps. In the early 1990s, an interest share like today's forced actual fiscal consolidation. The difference now: no captive creditors, no peace dividend, no productivity boom, and a far wider starting deficit.

**Slide 10**

The resolution of this story prints in specific documents. The third quarter GDP release either confirms public-held debt above 100 percent or it does not. The next two quarterly interest prints show whether $1,247 billion was the conveyor's top or a waypoint. Treasury refunding statements show whether buybacks become quiet yield management. And the NY Fed's next household debt report either verifies the 12.9 percent figure or retires it. Watch the bill, not the milestone.
