# India's Trade Deficit Blew Out by $17 Billion. The Headline Says Nothing Happened.

## A $3.1 billion current account print looks like calm; the composition underneath it is a single export engine and a central bank renting out its balance sheet.

Think of a household that loses a third of its salary to a sudden medical bill, yet ends the month with the same bank balance as before. The neighbours would call it resilience. The accountant would ask different questions: which credit card absorbed the shock, at what rate, and when does the bill arrive? India's latest balance of payments is that household. The bottom line barely moved. The lines above it moved enormously. What follows is an attempt to read those lines the way the accountant would, without panic and without comfort.

The plain version: a current account deficit means a country spends more abroad on goods, services, and investment income than it earns. In the April–June quarter of fiscal 2027, India's deficit was $3.1 billion — almost unchanged from a year earlier — even though its goods trade deficit widened by $16.8 billion to $85.7 billion. Two flows, services exports and remittances, absorbed nearly all of the difference. That composition, not the headline, is what this piece examines.

## What actually changed

In mid-August, the Reserve Bank of India published its balance of payments data for Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026), in the June 2026 release (Press Release 2026-27/894). India's current account deficit came in at $3.1 billion, against $2.9 billion in the same quarter a year earlier. On the surface, a rounding error of a change.

One line up, the surface breaks. The merchandise trade deficit, on a balance-of-payments basis, widened to $85.7 billion from $68.9 billion. Imports rose about 20% year-on-year to $218.0 billion, while exports actually grew about 17% to $132.2 billion. The gap between those two growth rates is mostly one commodity: crude oil, priced through a war. Since the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz following the February strikes on Iran, Brent has lived in a band between the high $80s and, in June, above $100 a barrel.

So the first fact is that India ran a $17 billion larger hole in goods trade and reported almost the same current account number. Something absorbed the difference. That something is the story.

## How the arithmetic actually works

India's current account is a three-pillar structure. The first pillar is a large, structural merchandise deficit, led by energy. The second is a surplus in services trade — software, IT, and business services sold largely to American corporates. The third is net transfers, which is mostly remittances from the diaspora. There is also a negative income account — investment income paid out to foreign investors — which shrank modestly to -$11.1 billion from -$12.8 billion.

In Q1 FY27, the arithmetic ran like this: -$85.7 billion in goods, offset by +$52.2 billion in net services and +$41.4 billion in net transfers. Net result: -$3.1 billion.

| Pillar | Q1 FY27 (USD bn) | Q1 FY26 (USD bn) |
|---|---|---|
| Merchandise trade (goods) | -85.7 | -68.9 |
| Net services exports | +52.2 | +47.9 |
| Net transfers (remittances) | +41.4 | +30.9 |
| Net income account | -11.1 | -12.8 |
| **Current account balance** | **-3.1** | **-2.9** |

*Source: RBI Press Release 2026-27/894, USD billion, balance-of-payments basis.*

Spend a moment on what those two offsets mean. Net services alone covered 61% of the goods deficit. Services and transfers together covered 109% of it. The current account headline is, quite literally, the residual of one sector's export performance and the diaspora's willingness to wire money home. Neither of those is a variable that trade policy can directly control, in the way that a gold import duty or an export subsidy can be adjusted in a notification.

This is what economists mean, without flourish, when they say the composition matters more than the level. A $3.1 billion deficit produced by balanced, diversified surpluses is a different animal from a $3.1 billion deficit produced by one engine and one remittance spike. The number is the same. The fragility is not.

## The remittance surprise, and why it deserves a raised eyebrow

The single largest swing against expectations was not services — services grew a trend-like 9.5% in gross terms, to $106.7 billion — but transfers. Net transfers jumped 34% year-on-year, from $30.9 billion to $41.4 billion. Bank of Baroda's chief economist, Madan Sabnavis, called the print "contrary to expectations," which in the measured dialect of bank economists is close to astonishment.

The plausible mechanism is exchange-rate arithmetic. The rupee had depreciated roughly 7% in the first half of 2026. Every dollar an expat wires home now converts into more rupees, which both raises the incentive to remit and pulls forward remittances that might otherwise have waited. It is a real flow, but a cyclical one: this channel strengthens precisely when the currency weakens, and it would fade if the rupee stabilised. RBI publishes no geographic or instrument breakdown of remittances at this lag, so the durability question is currently untestable in either direction. It is worth holding it as an open variable rather than a new structural fact.

## What happened on the capital account?

A balance of payments has two sides, and the capital account had its own quarter. It swung from a $7.4 billion surplus a year ago to a $5.0 billion deficit. The swing factor was portfolio flows: net foreign portfolio investment (FPI) went from +$1.6 billion to -$9.6 billion, as global risk re-priced around the war and US tariffs. Foreign direct investment, the slower and stickier money, actually improved — +$7.8 billion versus +$4.8 billion — and banking capital turned positive on NRI deposit inflows. A large negative "other capital" residual of -$10.1 billion, which includes errors and omissions, remains unexplained in the release.

Add the two accounts and the quarter printed an overall balance of payments deficit of $8.1 billion, against a $4.5 billion surplus a year earlier. Reserves fell by the same amount. That is worth stating plainly: the "contained CAD" quarter was, in cash terms, an $8.1 billion drawdown. The containment was a current-account property, not a reserves property.

June alone told a different story — capital account +$9.1 billion, overall BoP +$2.9 billion. The turn had already begun before the policy window opened. Which brings us to the window.

## How did the RBI engineer $72.8 billion of inflows in June?

On June 5 and 8, the RBI and the government announced a coordinated package to manufacture capital inflows. The centrepiece was a special USD-INR swap facility covering three channels: FCNR(B) deposits — dollar deposits that non-resident Indians place with Indian banks — plus external commercial borrowings and overseas foreign currency borrowings.

The mechanics repay a slow read. A bank raising a fresh three-to-five-year FCNR(B) deposit can swap the dollars with the RBI at par, receiving rupees today and committing to buy the dollars back at maturity at the same rate. The RBI absorbs the entire hedging cost — roughly 280 to 300 basis points a year at market rates — and exempts the deposits from cash reserve and statutory liquidity requirements. Freed of both costs, banks could offer NRIs dollar deposit rates of 5.5% to 7.1%, tax-free, at a moment when, on the market commentary of the period, a US money-market fund paid roughly 4%. The swaps are non-cancellable: the RBI carries the currency risk on its own balance sheet until 2029–2031.

Alongside the swap came tax changes: foreign investors were exempted from capital-gains and interest taxes on government securities, retrospective to April 1, and the fully accessible route was expanded to new 15-, 30-, and 40-year G-sec issuance. NRI equity investment limits were raised. Export-proceeds repatriation windows were relaxed.

The result, by RBI's own tally (PR 2026-27/948): $72.8 billion of inflows in eleven weeks, of which $65.4 billion came through the FCNR(B) channel alone. Portfolio flows returned — +$4.2 billion in July, +$2.3 billion in the first three weeks of August, per BusinessLine's reporting of NSDL data. Reserves rebuilt to about $707 billion by early August. On August 14, RBI closed the FCNR(B) leg early: deposits only till August 31, a month ahead of schedule.

Two observations belong here, stated as mechanism rather than judgment. First, none of this is "the market financing India." It is the sovereign converting its interest-rate differential into dollar inflows at a subsidised price, taking currency risk onto itself. The inflow is real; so is the liability. Second, the channel split is informative. The retail, subsidy-sensitive FCNR(B) leg drew $65.4 billion. The institutional ECB and OFCB legs drew $7.4 billion combined. Demand concentrated where the subsidy was richest.

## Three honest ways to read this

The first reading, close to the RBI's implicit position and to SBI Research's published view, is that this was a managed episode. The shock was external and price-driven; exports on both sides actually grew strongly; policy has restored the capital account; SBI now projects a FY27 BoP surplus of $5–10 billion with CAD at 1.5–1.7% of GDP — a reversal from its own earlier forecast of a $65–70 billion BoP deficit. The June data already turning positive supports this. Its vulnerability is that it leans on the remittance spike persisting and on crude behaving; July's customs-basis goods deficit of $32 billion, the worst since January, is already visible pressure on Q2.

The second reading is concentration risk. Services plus transfers now cover 109% of the goods deficit, which means the entire external account clears on the continued health of one export sector — one that is heavily exposed to US corporate tech spending (roughly 45%, on common estimates), facing visa and mobility friction, and newly exposed to AI-driven deflation of routine IT services work. There is no second hedge. Against this: the services surplus has grown through COVID and the 2022 oil spike alike, and diversification into global capability centres and business services may make the aggregate sturdier than "one engine" implies.

The third reading is that the balance of payments is now on borrowed time in a literal sense. The $65 billion of FCNR(B) money is three-to-five-year debt at 5.5–7.1% in dollars, with the RBI short the currency. The Q1 FPI outflow of $9.6 billion shows what organic appetite looked like before the inducements. Against this: the September 2013 precedent, an identical instrument, genuinely stabilised the rupee, and most of those deposits rolled over in 2016. That episode ended calmly partly because oil collapsed in 2014–15 and repaired the current account underneath the financing fix — a piece of luck the present framework is also, implicitly, hoping for.

None of these readings can be settled this quarter. What can be done is to state which data would settle them.

## What does the 2013 FCNR(B) window tell us — and not tell us?

The 2013 FCNR(B) window, opened during the taper tantrum, drew roughly $26 billion in three months — sources differ modestly, with SBI Research citing $24.5 billion — against a backdrop of 1–2% US rates and 9% Indian yields. The carry then was enormous. In 2026 the carry is thin: US short rates near 4%, Indian five-year yields around 6.4%. MUFG initially pencilled in perhaps $20 billion of uptake. Eleven weeks later the tally stood at $65.4 billion.

The lesson is not that history repeats. It is that in 2013 the binding constraint on NRI inflows was the interest-rate differential, and in 2026 it was the cost of hedging. The RBI identified the actual constraint and priced it to zero. That is competent central banking. It is also, as 2016 showed, a dated liability: the bill arrives on a schedule, and its size depends on where the rupee trades in 2029–2031.

## What this touches, and what it does not

For anyone making decisions, the transmission channels are concrete. IT services exporters gain on translation from a 95.7 rupee and carry the weight of the concentration question; their US deal commentary is now a macro variable, not just an equity one. Banks with large NRI franchises received $65 billion of low-effective-cost dollar funding, deployable into higher-yielding rupee assets — a margin gift through the end of the decade, with a contingent edge where leveraged FCNR structures were sold. Oil marketers and every energy-importing sector live on the other side of the goods deficit. Foreign ownership economics of Indian government debt changed structurally on April 1 — the retrospective tax exemption is a permanent alteration, not window dressing, and index-inclusion conversations (MUFG flags Bloomberg Global Aggregate) now have a different backdrop.

What this data does not tell you: what any specific asset will do. The rupee's level, equity multiples, and rate paths depend on variables — crude's settling point, US tech demand, the durability of the remittance spike — that are genuinely unresolved.

## The question the quarter leaves behind

India's external account currently balances on two narrow supports: one services engine on the current account, and one policy window on the capital account. Both have performed. The window closes August 31, and the deposits it gathered come due between 2029 and 2031.

So the question to carry forward is not whether the $3.1 billion print was good or bad. It is this: when the RBI's quarterly statement arrives with the full services composition — software versus business services versus travel — will it show the engine broadening, or the economy's most important hedge narrowing further into a single industry's order book? That number, not the headline, is the one worth remembering this quarter by.
