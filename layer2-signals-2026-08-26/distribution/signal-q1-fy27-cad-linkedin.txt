India reported a current account deficit of $3.1 billion last quarter. The trade deficit underneath that headline was $85.7 billion.

Both figures sit in the same RBI release. The merchandise gap widened by $16.8 billion year-on-year, driven by war-priced crude, while the current account barely moved. Something absorbed the difference, and the composition of that something is the actual story.

The mechanism: net services exports contributed +$52.2 billion and remittances +$41.4 billion, up 34 percent in a year. Together they covered 109 percent of the goods deficit. The headline number is whatever those two flows leave behind, and neither is a lever trade policy directly controls.

What makes this instance different is the other side of the ledger. The capital account swung from a $7.4 billion surplus to a $5.0 billion deficit as portfolio investors pulled $9.6 billion, so the "contained" quarter still drew down $8.1 billion of reserves. The June response was a dollar swap window where the RBI absorbed the full hedging cost on NRI deposits. It pulled in $72.8 billion in eleven weeks and was closed a month early. Those deposits are now liabilities maturing between 2029 and 2031, with the currency risk on the RBI's own book.

Who bears the cost if the single services engine slows: an external account with no second hedge.

The open question: when the RBI's quarterly statement publishes the full services composition, does it show the engine broadening, or the economy's most important hedge narrowing further into one industry's order book?
