# Fact-Check: From Four Marks to Four Trillion: The Weimar Hyperinflation

## Bottom line

The draft is historically solid. The major dates, exchange-rate figures, and character roles trace cleanly to the research artifact and to standard sources such as Britannica, the IMF, and Bresciani-Turroni. There are a few precision issues around the October 1923 exchange rate and one slightly overstated price anecdote. The draft already labels the most famous hyperinflation stories as probably exaggerated, which is good. Three items should be tightened before revision.

## Items to fix

| # | Claim in draft | Issue | Severity | Suggested fix | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "By October it was around 25 billion" marks to the dollar. | **Imprecise / disputed.** The research artifact contains two different October 1923 exchange-rate figures. Market Histories cites ~25.3 billion for October 1923, while the Reichsbank/Bresciani-Turroni table in the artifact cites 242 billion for October 1, 1923. The draft should not present one figure as settled. | Imprecise | Either drop the precise October figure and write "by October the rate was in the tens of billions," or note the source variation explicitly. Avoid implying a single authoritative October rate. | Research artifact key numbers / Coin & Empire vs. Reichsbank table |
| 2 | "A dozen eggs went from a few marks before the war to millions, then billions." | **Unsupported for the peak.** The research artifact confirms eggs at 0.5 marks in 1918, 3 marks in 1921, 500 marks in January 1923, and 30 million marks in September 1923. It does not confirm a November peak in the billions for eggs. | Unsupported | Hedge the peak: "A dozen eggs went from a few marks before the war to millions by September 1923; by November most food prices were quoted in billions or not accepted for marks at all." | Alpha History / Market Histories table in research artifact |
| 3 | "Some estimates put the daily cost of supporting the Ruhr at 40 million marks." | **Needs context.** The 40 million figure appears in one source in the artifact, but it is unclear whether this is in already-depreciated 1923 marks and at what point in the occupation. Without a date, the number is hard to interpret. | Imprecise | Add a date or caveat: "One contemporary estimate put the daily cost of Ruhr subsidies at 40 million marks early in the occupation; the nominal cost rose exponentially as the mark collapsed." | PDF source in research artifact (arxiujosepserradell.cat) |
| 4 | "The currency that bought 4.20 marks to the US dollar in 1914 now required 4.2 trillion marks to buy that same dollar." | **Technically true but framing could be tighter.** The 4.20 rate is the pre-war gold parity; by the time the war broke out the mark was already depreciating. The phrasing is fine, but note that 4.20 was the official parity, not necessarily the market rate for all of 1914. | Framing | Minor. Could add "official" or "pre-war parity" for precision. | Research artifact / Coin & Empire |

## Items that check out

- **August 1914 suspension of gold convertibility:** Confirmed by Deutsche Biographie / Havenstein entry.
- **London Ultimatum, May 5, 1921; Reichstag acceptance, May 11, 1921:** Confirmed by British Online Archives and Ray Dalio.
- **132 billion gold marks reparations bill, ~330% of GDP, ~10% of GDP annual service, ~80% of export earnings:** Confirmed by research artifact citing Ray Dalio.
- **Exchange-rate progression 4.20 (1914) → 64 (Jan 1921) → 493 (July 1922) → 17,972 (Jan 1923) → 4.2 trillion (Nov 1923):** Confirmed by Britannica, Coin & Empire, and Reichsbank table in research artifact.
- **Rathenau assassination, June 24, 1922:** Confirmed by research artifact.
- **Hyperinflation threshold crossed in mid-1922:** Confirmed by research artifact.
- **Ruhr occupation began January 11, 1923:** Confirmed by Ruhr Museum.
- **Passive resistance funded by money printing:** Confirmed by EBSCO source in research artifact.
- **Stresemann became chancellor August 13, 1923; ended passive resistance September 26, 1923:** Confirmed by Britannica and History Crunch.
- **Rentenmark introduced November 15, 1923; 1 Rentenmark = 1 trillion Papiermarks; 4.2 Rentenmarks = $1; 3.2 billion issuance ceiling:** Confirmed by Market Histories, Britannica, and IMF source in research artifact.
- **Hjalmar Schacht appointed Currency Commissioner November 12, 1923, then Reichsbank president after Havenstein’s death:** Confirmed by Market Histories and German History Docs source in research artifact.
- **Workers paid twice daily, prices revised multiple times daily, restaurants stopped printing menus:** Confirmed by Market Histories in research artifact.
- **Hugo Stinnes borrowed in depreciating marks to acquire real assets:** Confirmed by research artifact.
