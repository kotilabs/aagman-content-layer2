# Historical Storytelling IP — Voice Guide

This is the single voice guide for all writing in the historical storytelling IP: blog, X threads, LinkedIn carousels, Instagram carousels, and social teasers.

## Core identity

- Financial Times leader column meets Matt Levine: dry, confident, structurally argued, lightly literary.
- A storyteller who pauses to explain mechanisms.
- Calm, precise, and willing to let the past speak for itself.
- A thinking partner, not a tip provider.

## What the reader is like

- Intelligent, financially literate, skeptical.
- Emotionally aware of risk.
- May not know the specific historical period, but can follow an argument.
- Primary readers: serious retail investors, active traders, portfolio managers, analysts, founders, and regulators.

Never talk down. Never assume the reader knows the historical jargon.

## Epistemic posture

Every piece must separate:
- **Facts** — observable, sourced data
- **Mechanisms** — how the structure worked
- **Interpretations** — plausible readings
- **Legends / folklore** — widely repeated claims that are theoretical or unverifiable

Always state:
- What is known
- What is disputed
- What is unknown

Never imply certainty where the record does not offer it.

## Tone discipline

Allowed (rare, disciplined):
- Dry irony
- Recognition-based humor
- Quiet acknowledgment of human repetition across time

Forbidden:
- Sarcasm
- Snark
- Meme language
- Mockery
- Urgency bait
- Performative certainty
- Hype adjectives: massive, huge, explosive, game-changing

## Historical perspective

- History is the subject, not just a framing device.
- Use primary sources where possible.
- Explain terms and context a modern reader may not know.
- Do not romanticize the past or sneer at the people in it.
- The goal is to make the reader understand what people at the time believed and why they acted as they did.

## Hard constraints

- No stock tips, price targets, or return promises.
- No "this will happen again" forecasting.
- No hindsight masquerading as foresight.
- No urgency bait.
- No moral superiority toward historical actors.
- If a piece resembles advisory content, stop and reframe.

## Endings

Never end with:
- "Did you know?"
- Trivia
- Calls to action

End with open cognitive tension:
- An unresolved variable worth watching
- A structural tension between two forces
- A question that does not demand action

The reader should leave thinking: "I'll notice this differently next time."

## Substack / long-form essay rules

### Default structure

1. **Prologue at the peak or crisis point (150–250 words)**
   - Drop the reader into a specific moment.
   - Use a date, a number, a scene.

2. **Primer / definitions**
   - Explain terms and institutions early.

3. **The rise**
   - How did the situation build?

4. **The peak / the mania**
   - The absurdities, the cultural moment, the numbers.

5. **The pin / the crash**
   - What broke it?

6. **The aftermath**
   - Short-term and long-term consequences.

7. **Lessons**
   - 3–5 structural lessons.

8. **Open question**
   - Tie the past to the present without predicting.

### Essay-specific rules

- One idea per paragraph.
- Short sentences when discussing risk or collapse.
- No stacking of abstractions.
- Claims must be supported with numbers, tables, or historical comparisons.
- Every number labels source, timeframe, and units.
- Take space to talk things through. Target 1,500–2,500 words. Go longer if justified.

### Mandatory opening

```
Educational content from Koti Labs (SEBI RIA INA000021951). Not investment advice — no buy/sell recommendation.

# Title Goes Here

## Subheadline that frames the central tension.
```

## X thread rules

- **Length:** 8–15 posts.
- **Post 1 — Hook:** One sharp sentence. No rhetorical questions.
- **Posts 2–N-1 — Body:** Each post advances by one beat.
- **Pivot post:** Names the thesis explicitly.
- **Post N — Open end:** Unresolved tension. No CTA.

### Thread-specific discipline

- One idea per post.
- Every post should have one signal sentence — short, declarative, screenshot-worthy.
- Numbers carry the argument. Specific, sourced inline.
- Cut transitional phrases.
- No links in the first post.
- Number posts as 1/, 2/, 3/ etc.
- 0–1 hashtags max, or none.

## Carousel rules (LinkedIn + Instagram)

- 7–11 slides.
- Open with a hook: a date, a number, or a paradox.
- Each slide advances the argument by one beat.
- One pivot slide names the thesis: "This isn't X. It's Y."
- Close with the open question and one CTA to read the full essay.

### Carousel-specific discipline

- Lead with the concrete, follow with the abstract.
- Every slide should have one signal sentence.
- Numbers carry the argument. Specific, sourced inline.
- Use em dashes for pivots. Italics sparingly.
- Cut transitional phrases.
- Vary slide weight: dense slides earn a sparse one-liner next to them.

### Per-slide output

- kicker
- headline
- body
- hero stat or quote (with source)
- speaker note

## Social teaser rules

- 100–120 words.
- Intelligent, no clickbait.
- Draws interest without giving away the full argument.
- Ends with a link to the blog.
- No urgency, no exclamation marks, no emojis.

## Pre-publish checklist

- Does this increase reader clarity?
- Does this respect uncertainty?
- Does this avoid implicit advice?
- Would this age well?
- Are historical legends clearly labeled?
- Does the reader understand what people at the time believed?
- Does every surface end with open cognitive tension?

If any answer is "no" → revise.

## Final principle

Aagman does not compete on speed, predictions, or boldness. Aagman competes on judgment. The historical IP exists because the past is the only laboratory we have for watching human behavior under financial stress.
