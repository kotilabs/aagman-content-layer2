## Idea 1: wall-street-crash-1929

- **Title:** The Last Laugh of the Roaring Twenties: The Crash of 1929
- **Hook:** Nine days after Yale economist Irving Fisher said stocks had reached a "permanently high plateau," the Dow Jones fell 25% in four days.
- **Period / asset:** September 1929 – July 1932; US equities, especially the Dow Jones Industrial Average and leveraged investment trusts.
- **Why it matters now:** The crash is the template every modern bear market is measured against. It shows how leverage, mass participation, and the belief that "this time is different" can turn a correction into a collapse, and why the structure of the financial system matters as much as the mood.
- **Educational spine:**
  1. Margin debt can amplify a boom on the way up and turn a sell-off into a panic on the way down.
  2. Investment trusts were the 1920s version of layered financial engineering, hiding risk behind complexity.
  3. Crashes rarely have one cause; they happen when several vulnerabilities line up at once.
- **Core characters / forces:** Irving Fisher, Charles Mitchell (National City Bank), Jesse Livermore, Roger Babson, the Federal Reserve, Goldman Sachs Trading Corporation, retail margin buyers.
- **Suggested surfaces:** blog, thread, LinkedIn carousel, Instagram carousel, infographic concepts.
- **Source confidence:** high
- **Viability note:** Extensively documented through exchange records, contemporary newspapers, Senate hearings, and later histories; clear narrative arc and strong character roster.

## Idea 2: asian-financial-crisis-1997

- **Title:** Pegged to the Edge: The Asian Financial Crisis, 1997
- **Hook:** Thailand burned through roughly $33 billion of foreign reserves defending its currency peg — then ran out, and the baht collapsed on July 2, 1997.
- **Period / asset:** May 1997 – 1998; currencies, equities, and real estate across Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia, and Hong Kong.
- **Why it matters now:** It is the clearest modern case study of how fixed exchange rates, foreign-currency debt, and crony-capital balance sheets can turn a local problem into a regional contagion. The same pressure points reappear in emerging markets today.
- **Educational spine:**
  1. A currency peg works until markets believe the central bank cannot defend it.
  2. Borrowing in foreign currency while earning in local currency is a balance-sheet mismatch that can wipe out solvent-looking companies overnight.
  3. Contagion travels fast when investors treat a region as one trade.
- **Core characters / forces:** Bank of Thailand, Prime Minister Chavalit Yongchaiyudh, George Soros / Quantum Fund, Mahathir Mohamad, the IMF, Korean chaebols, Hong Kong Monetary Authority, Suharto's Indonesia.
- **Suggested surfaces:** blog, thread, LinkedIn carousel, Instagram carousel, infographic concepts.
- **Source confidence:** high
- **Viability note:** Excellent contemporary coverage, IMF reports, central bank data, and named institutional actors; the timeline is tight and the mechanism is clean.

## Idea 3: dot-com-bubble-1995-2000

- **Title:** Profits Optional: The Dot-Com Bubble, 1995–2000
- **Hook:** The NASDAQ rose from under 750 in 1995 to 5,048 in March 2000 — then fell 77% over the next two and a half years.
- **Period / asset:** 1995 – March 2000; US technology and internet equities, NASDAQ Composite.
- **Why it matters now:** It is the modern archetype of a narrative-driven mania: a real technological revolution, yes, but with stock prices discounted to a fantasy future. The lesson repeats whenever a new platform shifts attention before earnings catch up.
- **Educational spine:**
  1. A good story is not the same as a good business; revenue and a path to profit still matter.
  2. Retail access and media enthusiasm can detach prices from fundamentals for years.
  3. When capital is free, business discipline disappears; when capital tightens, it returns overnight.
- **Core characters / forces:** Alan Greenspan and the Federal Reserve, Pets.com, Cisco Systems, AOL Time Warner, day traders, venture capital firms, the NASDAQ index.
- **Suggested surfaces:** blog, thread, LinkedIn carousel, Instagram carousel, infographic concepts.
- **Source confidence:** high
- **Viability note:** Strong primary data through SEC filings, IPO prospectuses, and contemporaneous business press; the rise-and-fall structure is well-defined and the cast is recognizable.

## Notes for operator

All three ideas passed the external viability check with multiple independent sources confirming dates and numbers. The 1929 crash and the Asian crisis are heavier systemic stories; the dot-com bubble is more character-driven and may be the easiest entry point for readers new to the IP.
