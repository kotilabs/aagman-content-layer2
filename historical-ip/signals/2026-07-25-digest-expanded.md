## Idea 1: black-monday-1987

- **Title:** The Day the Machines Took the Wheel: Black Monday, 1987
- **Hook:** On October 19, 1987, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 22.6% in a single session — still the largest one-day drop in US history.
- **Period / asset:** October 1987; US equities, global stock markets, futures and options markets.
- **Why it matters now:** It is the canonical example of how risk-management tools can amplify the very risks they are supposed to control. Portfolio insurance, designed to limit losses, ended up forcing synchronized selling across markets.
- **Educational spine:**
  1. Hedging strategies can become self-fulfilling prophecies when everyone uses them at once.
  2. Liquidity disappears exactly when every automated seller is trying to get out.
  3. A crash does not need a recession or a scandal; sometimes structure is enough.
- **Core characters / forces:** Alan Greenspan and the Federal Reserve, portfolio insurers (Leland-O'Brien-Rubinstein), the NYSE and futures exchanges, Treasury Secretary James Baker, program traders, index arbitrageurs.
- **Suggested surfaces:** blog, thread, LinkedIn carousel, Instagram carousel, infographic concepts.
- **Source confidence:** high
- **Viability note:** Extensively documented by the Brady Report, SEC studies, exchange records, and contemporary business press; the single-day collapse gives it a tight narrative spine.

## Idea 2: global-financial-crisis-2008

- **Title:** The House That Subprime Built: The Global Financial Crisis, 2008
- **Hook:** On September 15, 2008, Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy — a 158-year-old investment bank undone by mortgages its own traders barely understood.
- **Period / asset:** 2007–2009; US housing, mortgage-backed securities, CDOs, global banking equities.
- **Why it matters now:** It is the most recent full-scale systemic crisis and the best-documented example of how complexity, leverage, and misplaced confidence in models can hide risk until it is too late.
- **Educational spine:**
  1. Securitization can spread risk so thin that no one knows who is holding it.
  2. Rating agencies and risk models can fail at the same time, for the same reason.
  3. When housing prices stop rising, the entire leverage chain unwinds from the bottom up.
- **Core characters / forces:** Lehman Brothers, Bear Stearns, AIG, Goldman Sachs, the Federal Reserve, Treasury Secretary Hank Paulson, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, mortgage originators, credit rating agencies.
- **Suggested surfaces:** blog, thread, LinkedIn carousel, Instagram carousel, infographic concepts.
- **Source confidence:** high
- **Viability note:** Abundant primary material: FCIC report, bankruptcy filings, congressional testimony, contemporaneous journalism; the timeline is well-established and the cast is large.

## Idea 3: south-sea-bubble-1720

- **Title:** The Company That Ate the National Debt: The South Sea Bubble, 1720
- **Hook:** In six months, shares of the South Sea Company rose from 128 to over 1,000 — then collapsed back below 200 by year-end.
- **Period / asset:** 1711–1720; shares of the South Sea Company, British government debt.
- **Why it matters now:** It is the first modern financial bubble: a public company, a debt-conversion scheme, bribery in Parliament, and ordinary investors ruined by a story that sounded too good to check.
- **Educational spine:**
  1. A scheme to reduce government debt can become a vehicle for speculation if it is also a stock promotion.
  2. Political connections and financial innovation are a dangerous combination.
  3. When a bubble bursts, the legal and political aftermath can last longer than the bubble itself.
- **Core characters / forces:** Robert Harley, John Blunt, the South Sea Company directors, Sir Isaac Newton, the Earl of Sunderland, the Bank of England, Parliament.
- **Suggested surfaces:** blog, thread, LinkedIn carousel, Instagram carousel, infographic concepts.
- **Source confidence:** high
- **Viability note:** Well-documented through Parliamentary inquiries, company records, contemporary pamphlets and satires, and recent scholarship; the price series and bribery investigations are established.

## Idea 4: hunt-brothers-silver-corner-1979-1980

- **Title:** Silver Thursday: The Last Great Commodity Corner, 1979–1980
- **Hook:** Two Texas oil heirs accumulated so much silver that by January 1980 the price hit $50.35 an ounce — then lost half its value in a single day on March 27.
- **Period / asset:** 1979–1980; silver spot and futures markets.
- **Why it matters now:** It is the cleanest modern example of what happens when a leveraged corner meets an exchange that can change the rules mid-game.
- **Educational spine:**
  1. A corner only works while you can keep buying and the exchanges let you.
  2. Leverage turns a winning position into a forced seller overnight.
  3. Regulators and exchanges move slowly until they move very fast.
- **Core characters / forces:** Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt, COMEX, CBOT, the CFTC, Saudi partners, major silver dealers, banks financing the position.
- **Suggested surfaces:** blog, thread, LinkedIn carousel, Instagram carousel, infographic concepts.
- **Source confidence:** high
- **Viability note:** Documented through exchange records, CFTC reports, bankruptcy proceedings, and contemporary press coverage; the price spike and "Silver Thursday" collapse are well-established.

## Idea 5: british-railway-mania-1840s

- **Title:** The Railway King: Britain's Victorian Tech Bubble, 1843–1847
- **Hook:** At its peak, railway investment consumed an estimated 7% of British GDP — and Parliament had approved enough new lines to cross the country several times over.
- **Period / asset:** 1843–1847; British railway company shares, parliamentary railway Acts.
- **Why it matters now:** It is the original technology-infrastructure mania: a genuinely transformative innovation, a financing boom, overbuilding, accounting fraud, and a collapse that took decades to absorb.
- **Educational spine:**
  1. Transformative technology and a good investment are not the same thing.
  2. Overbuilding is what turns a boom into a bust.
  3. When credit tightens, even transformative projects can become stranded assets.
- **Core characters / forces:** George Hudson (the "Railway King"), Parliament and the railway Acts, the Bank of England, railway promoters and engineers, provincial investors.
- **Suggested surfaces:** blog, thread, LinkedIn carousel, Instagram carousel, infographic concepts.
- **Source confidence:** high
- **Viability note:** Strong documentation in Parliamentary records, contemporary financial press, and recent economic-history scholarship; some GDP-investment estimates vary but the boom-bust arc is clear.

## Idea 6: tulip-mania-1636-1637

- **Title:** Wind Trade: Tulip Mania in the Dutch Republic, 1636–1637
- **Hook:** At the peak, a single rare tulip bulb could cost more than a skilled craftsman's annual salary — and the market collapsed within weeks in early 1637.
- **Period / asset:** 1634–1637; tulip bulbs and futures contracts in the Dutch Republic.
- **Why it matters now:** It is the most famous bubble in history and a useful case study in how futures contracts, scarcity, and social contagion can detach prices from any underlying value.
- **Educational spine:**
  1. Scarcity plus leverage — even informal leverage — can create explosive price moves.
  2. Futures contracts let traders speculate without ever owning the underlying asset.
  3. Bubbles can burst even when nothing fundamental changes.
- **Core characters / forces:** Dutch merchants and weavers, tulip brokers in Haarlem and Amsterdam, the Dutch East India Company and the new merchant class, varieties like Semper Augustus.
- **Suggested surfaces:** blog, thread, LinkedIn carousel, Instagram carousel, infographic concepts.
- **Source confidence:** medium
- **Viability note:** The episode is famous but the price record is patchy and modern historians disagree on how widespread the losses were; enough documented material exists for a story, but it requires careful sourcing.
