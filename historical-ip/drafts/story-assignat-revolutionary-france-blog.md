# The Sound of Breaking Plates
## How Revolutionary France Printed Its Future Away

### Prologue: The Funeral in the Square

In February 1796, workmen dragged heavy metal plates into the Place Vendôme in Paris and smashed them with sledgehammers while a crowd looked on. The government called it a ceremony of national recovery. Everyone else knew it was an admission of defeat.

Those plates had printed the assignat — the paper money that, for six years, paid French soldiers, bought bread, settled debts, and redistributed the land of the Church. By the time the hammers came down, the notes were worth less than half a percent of what was written on them.

The assignats were not destroyed by bad paper or weak collateral. They were printed on sturdy stock, signed by clerks, stamped with seals, and backed by abbeys and vineyards that were very real. What destroyed them was simpler: the people issuing them kept changing the deal. A bond became currency. Currency became a tax token. A token backed by land became a token backed by nothing but the next issue. By the end, the notes still looked like money. They just no longer behaved like it.

That gap — between what a note promises and what a government actually does — is what this story is about.

### A Few Things to Know Before We Begin

A quick primer, because the rest of this story will make more sense if the names and numbers are clear from the start.

**Assignat** — the paper money at the centre of this story. The word comes from the French for “assigned.” It was originally a claim on confiscated Church land. Over six years it became ordinary currency, then worthless currency.

**Livre** — the French unit of account, similar to a pound. Think of it as the franc’s predecessor. Prices, debts, and government budgets were all measured in livres.

**Specie** — gold and silver coins. This was “real” money throughout the period. When people lost faith in paper, they quoted prices in specie.

**Estates-General** — the assembly Louis XVI called in 1789 to solve the debt crisis. It had not met in 175 years. When it deadlocked, the commoners broke away and formed the National Assembly.

**National Assembly** — the revolutionary parliament that ran France from 1789 to 1791, later replaced by the Legislative Assembly and then the Convention.

**Church property, or biens nationaux** — the land, buildings, and estates confiscated from the Catholic Church starting in November 1789. This was the collateral behind the assignat.

**Face value** — the number printed on a note. A 100-livre assignat always said “100 livres.” What it could actually buy was a different question.

With that in hand, the story begins in earnest.

### The Starting Problem: A Country with Wealth, but No Cash

France in 1788 was not poor. It was insolvent. Decades of war, most recently France’s support for the American Revolution, had left the Crown owing something like four or five billion livres. Roughly half of everything the government collected went straight back out as debt payments.

The tax system was a joke, but not a funny one. Peasants and townspeople paid heavily. Clergy and nobility mostly did not. When Louis XVI called the Estates-General in May 1789, he was not looking for a debate about liberty. He was looking for a way to avoid default.

Then the revolution made the math worse. On the night of August 4, 1789, the Assembly abolished feudal dues, tithes, and a bundle of indirect taxes. Morally, this was one of the great moments of the revolution. Financially, it was like quitting your job before you have found another one.

Necker, the king’s finance minister, saw what was coming. He warned that new loans would only dig the hole deeper. In August 1789 he tried to borrow anyway — first at 4.5%, then at 5%. Nobody lent. By September the treasury was empty.

The new France inherited a bankrupt state, a broken tax machine, and a political class that had just shown it could tear institutions down faster than it could build them.

### The Asset Grab

The answer came from Talleyrand, the Bishop of Autun — not exactly the person you would expect to nationalise the Church. On November 2, 1789, the National Assembly voted 568 to 346 to put all Church property “at the disposal of the nation.”

The scale was absurd. The Church owned perhaps 260,000 buildings and 4.7 million hectares of land — maybe 6 to 10 percent of France. Estimates of the value ran from two to three billion livres, with some as high as four billion. Bishops’ palaces, abbeys, convents, forests, vineyards, farms, urban rents — fifteen centuries of accumulated real estate were now sitting in the state’s lap.

There was a political angle too. Sell the land to farmers and townspeople, and you create a new middle class whose loyalty is tied to the revolutionary government that gave them title. The regime would be paid for, and paid for by its enemies.

There was just one catch. You cannot feed Paris or pay an army with a cathedral. Real estate is illiquid. The revolution needed cash, and it needed it fast. So it did what broke regimes have done ever since: it printed claims against the assets.

On December 19, 1789, the assignat was born.

### The Good Idea

The first assignats were not money as we think of it. They were bonds. Interest-bearing certificates, first at 5%, later at 3%. Big denominations: 1,000, 300, and 200 livres. They carried the portrait of Louis XVI, patriotic symbols, and the daily interest printed in the margin. They were signed, countersigned, watermarked, and stamped. This was serious-looking paper.

The mechanism was clever. If you wanted to buy confiscated Church land at auction, you paid in assignats. When the sale closed, the state cancelled the notes and burned them. The word “assignat” comes from “assigned” — assigned to a specific piece of national property. As land sold, the paper disappeared. In theory, the system would liquidate itself: land sold, notes burned, problem solved.

For a while, that is exactly what happened. From December 1789 to December 1791, the amount of assignats in circulation rose from 400 million to about 1.36 billion livres. Yet the paper lost only about 4.3% of its value against gold and silver. Trade picked up. The treasury met its bills. Andrew Dickson White, the American historian who studied the episode a century later, wrote that the first result “was apparently all that the most sanguine could desire.”

The warning voices were there, but they were drowned out. Necker resigned in 1790 and left France. Mirabeau, the great orator of the Assembly, privately called paper money “a nursery of tyranny, corruption and delusion,” then publicly endorsed more of it to “save the country.” Brillat-Savarin, the deputy who warned that the second issue would knock 30% off the value, is remembered today not as a prophet but as the man who wrote *The Physiology of Taste*.

The machine only worked if the rules stayed fixed. They did not.

### The First Rule Changes

Think of the original assignat as a gift card backed by land. You could use it to buy Church property, and once the property sold, the card expired. That was the deal. Then the Assembly started changing the deal.

In April 1790, assignats were declared legal tender. Now you had to accept them for anything, not just land.

In September 1790, the state said you could pay taxes in assignats — but it did not burn the notes it received. So instead of retiring paper, the treasury recycled it.

In October 1790, the interest payments stopped. The assignat was no longer a bond. It was currency.

Each change made the paper more convenient and less trustworthy. Each widened the gap between the number on the note and the land supposedly behind it.

Then came war. On April 20, 1792, France declared war on Austria. Prussia joined in. The real deficit exploded — roughly tenfold. Tax collection collapsed. By February 1795 taxes covered only 6.4% of what the government spent. The Assembly could not borrow, because no one would lend. It could not tax enough. So it printed.

The link to land sales was effectively broken. The assignat became a promise that the next issue would be the last. That promise was broken every few months.

### When the Guillotine Became Monetary Policy

By 1793 the revolution was fighting for survival. Foreign armies were pressing in. The Vendée, a rural region in the west, had risen against conscription and Paris. The moderates were gone. The Jacobins, led by Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety, ruled through terror.

Their answer to rising prices was force. On September 29, 1793, the Convention passed the Law of General Maximum. It fixed prices for grain, flour, meat, firewood, soap, salt, tobacco, and more. Wages were capped at one and a half times their 1790 level.

The penalties escalated fast. Selling gold or silver coin, refusing assignats, accepting them at a discount, or even asking “in what money?” before a deal could mean fines, prison in irons, or death.

For about a year, official prices stayed down. But an official price is not the same as a loaf on the shelf. Farmers hid grain. Merchants sold only on the black market. The assignat kept circulating because the guillotine stood behind it, not because anyone believed in it.

To make things worse, the British and royalist émigrés were running a counterfeiting operation. Workshops in London, Holland, and Switzerland churned out forged assignats. One seizure at Passy in March 1792 uncovered 13 million livres of fake notes. After that, no one could tell how much of the inflation was printed in Paris and how much was printed by the enemy.

Even the rebels got stuck. When Vendéen forces captured an assignat press at Thouars in 1793, they found that local peasants and merchants refused paper money. So the rebel council — which had taken up arms against the revolution — ended up decreeing forced circulation of assignats. Once you invent this machine, everyone has to use it.

### The Deluge

Robespierre fell on July 27, 1794. With him went the Terror, and with the Terror went the enforcement behind the Maximum. In December 1794 the Convention repealed price controls. The market, suddenly free to price things honestly, spoke loudly.

The collapse was astonishing. By one estimate, an assignat in Paris was worth roughly 18% of face value in early 1795, about 3% by July, below 1% by October, and something like a quarter to half of one percent by the time the plates were smashed in 1796.

| Approximate value of an assignat in Paris | |
|---|---|
| Early 1795 | ~18% of face value |
| July 1795 | ~3% |
| October 1795 | Less than 1% |
| February 1796 | ~0.25–0.5% |

Circulation went from 400 million livres in 1789 to roughly 45 billion by 1796. At the peak, the government was making enough profit from printing to cover more than the entire 1790 state budget. Economists call this profit seigniorage. What it meant in practice was simple: France was paying for war by silently taxing anyone who held cash.

Daily life turned into a race. Workers demanded daily wages and spent them the same evening, because prices would be higher tomorrow. Merchants quoted prices in gold or silver but accepted paper at the going discount. Savings disappeared. Debtors paid old loans in worthless paper and walked away clean. The careful, the widowed, the salaried, and the rural poor were ruined. Speculators and the connected grew rich.

This was the hidden transfer. The revolution had abolished feudal dues, only to invent a new way of moving wealth from the weak to the clever and the well-placed.

### The Failed Cures

The Directory, which took over in November 1795, knew the assignat was dead. In December it ordered a forced loan of 600 million livres from the rich, accepting assignats at about 1% of face. In February 1796 it staged the plate-smashing in the Place Vendôme, as if breaking the machine would break the habit.

Then it tried the same trick again.

The replacement was the *mandat territorial*, another paper currency backed by national land, exchangeable at thirty assignats for one mandat. It collapsed too. By March 1796 assignats were demonetised. By February 1797 France was back on metallic money and had repudiated two-thirds of the national debt.

The cycle was complete. The revolution had started by confiscating Church land to solve a debt crisis. It ended by confiscating the savings of its own people through inflation, then defaulting on what was left.

### Napoleon’s Gold

The return to sound money did not come from another revolutionary idea. It came from Napoleon.

In 1799 he overthrew the Directory. In 1800 he created the Bank of France, partly as a private bank whose shareholders had a direct interest in not debasing the currency. In 1803 he established the *franc germinal*, a gold-silver standard. The currency held because the state finally looked stable enough to collect taxes, enforce contracts, and leave the printing press alone.

Napoleon’s regime was many things, but it was not romantic about paper money. It understood the basic point the revolution had missed: a currency is credible not because it is pretty, or land-backed, or legal tender, but because the issuer keeps its promises. The assignat failed because the Assembly kept rewriting the contract between the note and the nation.

### Why This Still Matters

The assignat is usually treated as a footnote to the French Revolution — a monetary sideshow between the Tennis Court Oath and the guillotine. That is wrong. For six years it was the revolution’s engine. It paid for the war, moved the land, and finally turned many ordinary French people against the regime that claimed to be liberating them.

The lessons are simple, but they keep being forgotten.

**Money is a promise.** The assignat was beautiful, well-printed, and backed by prime French real estate. None of that saved it once the promise changed.

**Collateral only matters if you honor it.** The Church lands were real. The commitment to retire assignats as the lands sold was not. Once the state accepted assignats for taxes without burning them, and once it started printing to pay for war, the land behind the notes became window dressing.

**Price controls do not create supply.** The Maximum could pin down the official price of bread. It could not bake more bread. It just pushed trade into the shadows.

**Inflation is a tax, and it hits the wrong people.** It moves wealth from savers, wage-earners, and the poor to debtors, speculators, and the connected. The assignat was a wealth tax collected not by any treasury official but by the printing press.

**Credibility is the real backing.** Britain, fighting the same wars, suspended gold convertibility in 1797 but did not default. It returned to gold in 1819 at the old rate. France defaulted, demonetised its paper, and spent a generation rebuilding trust.

When the plates were smashed in the Place Vendôme, the sound was meant to close the book. It also opened a century of French suspicion toward paper money. The lesson was expensive: a currency is only as good as the institutions behind it. And institutions, like revolutions, are judged not by what they promise, but by what they do.
