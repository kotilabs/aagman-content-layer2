# From Four Marks to Four Trillion: The Weimar Hyperinflation

## A currency collapse is not a natural disaster. It is a chain of decisions — and Germany made them for two years.

---

### Prologue: November 1923

On a morning in early November 1923, a factory worker in Berlin collected his wages at the end of his shift. By the time he reached the baker on the corner, the price of a loaf of bread had changed. Again. He paid anyway, because the alternative was to hold paper whose value was dissolving between breakfast and lunch.

This was daily life in Germany. Workers were often paid twice a day — once at midday, once in the evening — so that families could spend the morning’s wages before the afternoon’s price revisions made them worthless. Shopkeepers stopped printing prices on menus and tags; it was faster to write them on chalkboards. Farmers refused to sell grain for marks. City dwellers bartered pocket watches, furniture, and shoes for food. A currency that had stood at an official parity of 4.20 marks to the US dollar before the war now required 4.2 trillion marks to buy that same dollar.

The numbers are so large they become cartoonish. But the crisis was not a cartoon. It was the endpoint of a political and monetary process that had begun nearly a decade earlier, when Germany decided to fight a war on credit and never found a way back to sound money.

---

### Primer: a few things worth knowing

To understand what happened, it helps to know the vocabulary the actors used.

The **Reichsbank** was Germany’s central bank. Before 1914 it was legally obliged to redeem its banknotes in gold. In August 1914, at the start of World War I, that obligation was suspended. It was never restored.

The **Papiermark** was the paper mark — the currency Germany actually used after 1914. It had no gold backing. It was, in the end, a piece of paper with a promise on it.

The **Goldmark** was a unit of account tied to gold. Reparations were denominated in gold marks, not paper marks. This mattered because the gap between what Germany owed and what its paper currency could buy in foreign exchange kept widening.

**Reparations** were the payments demanded by the Allied powers under the Treaty of Versailles to cover war damage. They were not a bookkeeping fiction. They were a hard-currency claim on a country whose own currency was increasingly soft.

The **London Ultimatum** of May 1921 fixed Germany’s reparations bill at 132 billion gold marks — roughly 330 percent of German GDP — and demanded annual payments of around 2 billion gold marks plus a share of exports. That debt-service burden consumed about 10 percent of GDP and roughly 80 percent of export earnings.

The **Rentenmark** was the temporary currency introduced in November 1923 to replace the Papiermark. It was nominally backed by a mortgage on German land and industry. In practice, its value rested on a strict issuance ceiling and the public’s belief that the printing press had been locked away.

---

### The rise: from war finance to peace without peace

Germany entered World War I with a gold-backed currency and left it with a habit. The government did not want to raise taxes enough to pay for the war, so it borrowed. When borrowing was not enough, the Reichsbank printed. By November 1918, when the war ended, the currency was already wounded.

The wound did not heal in peacetime. The new Weimar Republic inherited a depleted economy, a demoralized population, and the Treaty of Versailles. The treaty imposed territorial losses, military restrictions, and reparations. It also included Article 231, the so-called war-guilt clause, which made the payments politically poisonous inside Germany.

At first, the situation looked manageable. The currency depreciated, but not catastrophically. In January 1921, the dollar traded at about 64 paper marks. That was a sixteen-fold devaluation from the pre-war rate, but it was not yet hyperinflation. Germany was inflating, but it was not yet collapsing.

Then came the London Ultimatum. The Allies demanded that Germany accept the 132 billion gold mark bill within six days or face occupation of the Ruhr, the industrial heartland. The Reichstag accepted on May 11, 1921. It is worth pausing on what that meant. Germany had committed to pay an external debt denominated in gold, using a domestic currency that it could print but that foreigners would not accept at anything close to the old parity.

There were only three ways to close the gap: tax more, borrow more, or print more. Taxing more was politically difficult and economically risky in a weak post-war economy. Borrowing more was possible only if lenders believed Germany could repay. Printing was the path of least resistance. It was also the path that led to the abyss.

---

### The acceleration: 1922

Hyperinflation, by the conventional definition, begins when monthly inflation exceeds 50 percent. Germany crossed that threshold in the second half of 1922.

Several things happened at once. The assassination of Foreign Minister Walther Rathenau in June 1922 by right-wing extremists triggered capital flight. Domestic and foreign investors who had been willing to hold marks began to dump them. The exchange rate, which had been around 190 marks to the dollar in January 1922, reached roughly 7,000 by the end of the year. Prices followed.

The Reichsbank, under President Rudolf Havenstein, responded by doing more of what it had already been doing. It discounted Treasury bills — that is, it printed money to buy government IOUs — on a scale that expanded almost exponentially. Havenstein did not see himself as a monetary arsonist. He believed, or told himself, that the inflation was caused by reparations, by speculators, by the Ruhr crisis, by anything except the banknote issuance he controlled.

This is one of the most important features of the period. The people in charge were not stupid. They were trapped in a structure. The government needed money to pay reparations, civil servants, soldiers, and the unemployed. The Reichsbank was legally and politically subordinate to the government. And the public, seeing prices rise, learned to spend marks as quickly as possible. Each actor’s rational response made the aggregate worse.

---

### The peak: the Ruhr and the mania

The crisis became a collapse in 1923. On January 11, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr valley, Germany’s coal and steel region, after Germany fell behind on reparations deliveries. The German government, unable to resist militarily, called for passive resistance. Workers were told not to cooperate with the occupiers. The government promised to keep paying their wages.

There was only one way to do that: print marks.

The cost was staggering. One contemporary estimate put the daily cost of Ruhr subsidies at 40 million marks early in the occupation; the nominal cost rose exponentially as the mark collapsed. The Reichsbank’s note presses ran at capacity. The money supply, which had already expanded enormously, exploded. By July 1923 the dollar stood at about 353,000 paper marks. By October the rate was in the tens of billions. On November 20, the official rate reached roughly 4.2 trillion marks to the dollar.

The numbers are almost impossible to feel. So consider what they meant in practice.

A loaf of bread that cost around 250 marks in January 1923 cost roughly 200 billion marks by November. A dozen eggs went from a few marks before the war to 30 million by September 1923; by November most food prices were quoted in billions or not accepted for marks at all. Workers were paid twice a day because wages set in the morning would not buy dinner. Wives met husbands at factory gates at noon to take the morning pay and run to the shops. Shopkeepers revised prices multiple times a day. Restaurants stopped printing menus.

Some of the most vivid stories — the thief who stole a suitcase but left the cash, the woman who carried banknotes in a wheelbarrow — are widely repeated and probably exaggerated in detail. But the underlying behavior was real. When money loses value by the hour, people stop treating it as a store of value. They buy anything tangible: food, foreign currency, furniture, stocks of coal. Velocity — the speed at which money changes hands — soared.

This is the part of the story that modern readers often miss. Hyperinflation is not simply a lot of money printing. It is a lot of money printing combined with a collapse in the demand to hold money. Once the public decides that cash is a hot potato, the central bank’s balance sheet becomes almost irrelevant. The same stock of money, passed faster and faster, chases the same shrinking supply of goods.

---

### The pin: when money stops being money

There was no single pin. There was a feedback loop that kept accelerating until something broke.

The loop worked like this. The government had expenses it could not cover with taxes. It issued Treasury bills. The Reichsbank bought them with newly created marks. The new marks pushed down the exchange rate and pushed up prices. Workers demanded higher wages. Businesses raised prices. The government needed even more marks to meet its now-larger nominal obligations. So it issued more bills. The Reichsbank printed more money.

Each round made the next round necessary. And each round eroded confidence further. By late 1923, farmers would not accept paper marks for produce. Industrial workers demanded payment in kind or in foreign currency. The middle class, whose savings were in marks, was wiped out. Pensioners found their monthly payments worthless.

The winners were those who owed marks and owned real things. Hugo Stinnes, an industrialist, borrowed heavily in depreciating currency to buy coal mines, steel mills, and shipping lines. He became one of the richest men in Europe. His debts melted away; his assets did not. This is one of the cruel features of hyperinflation: it redistributes wealth arbitrarily, rewarding leverage and punishing thrift.

Politically, the republic began to crack. The Ruhr occupation had not forced France out. The economy was in free fall. In August 1923, Chancellor Wilhelm Cuno resigned. Gustav Stresemann took over and, in September, called off passive resistance. The immediate fiscal drain stopped. But the currency was already beyond saving.

---

### The aftermath: the Rentenmark and the art of credibility

The stabilization that followed is, in some ways, more surprising than the collapse.

On November 15, 1923, the government introduced the Rentenmark. One Rentenmark exchanged for one trillion Papiermarks. The rate to the dollar was fixed at 4.2 Rentenmarks — the same pre-war parity. The total issuance was capped at 3.2 billion Rentenmarks.

The Rentenmark was nominally backed by a mortgage on German land and industry. In truth, that backing was largely a confidence device. The land was not going to be sold to redeem notes. What made the Rentenmark credible was the combination of a hard issuance ceiling and a new set of policymakers who acted as if the printing press had been retired.

Hjalmar Schacht, appointed Currency Commissioner and soon Reichsbank president, refused to let the Reichsbank discount further government bills. Stresemann’s government cut spending and introduced new taxes. The budget, which had been financed by the printing press, was brought closer to balance.

The effect was rapid. Prices stopped rising. The dollar exchange rate held. Within months, the Rentenmark had replaced the Papiermark as the practical unit of account. In 1924, the Rentenmark was itself replaced by the Reichsmark, at one-to-one.

It is tempting to say the Rentenmark “worked.” A more precise statement is that the policy regime changed enough for people to believe it would work. A currency is a coordination game. Once enough people believe the unit will hold value, their behavior — holding it, pricing in it, lending in it — makes that belief self-fulfilling.

---

### Lessons

**1. Hyperinflation is a fiscal phenomenon dressed up as a monetary one.**

The Reichsbank printed the notes, but the root cause was a government that could not match its spending to its revenues. The printing press was the symptom. The fiscal gap was the disease.

**2. A currency’s value rests on the belief that it will hold value.**

Money is not valuable because of the paper it is printed on. It is valuable because people expect others to accept it tomorrow. When that expectation breaks, velocity rises and the same stock of money produces ever-higher prices.

**3. Stabilization requires a credible rule, not just a new currency.**

The Rentenmark was not magical. It worked because it was issued in limited quantity and because the government stopped financing itself through the printing press. Without that fiscal discipline, a new currency would have collapsed just like the old one.

**4. Inflation redistributes wealth arbitrarily.**

Savers, pensioners, and wage workers who could not index their incomes fast enough were ruined. Debtors with real assets, like Hugo Stinnes, profited. Hyperinflation is not a neutral rise in the price level. It is a transfer of wealth from creditors to debtors, from the cautious to the leveraged.

**5. External pressure can trigger a crisis, but domestic policy determines whether it becomes a catastrophe.**

The reparations burden was real and heavy. The Ruhr occupation was a genuine shock. But other countries have faced large external debts and occupation without producing hyperinflation. Germany’s policy choice — to print rather than tax, borrow, or default openly — was what turned pressure into collapse.

---

### Open question

Why did the Rentenmark hold value when its land backing was largely a fiction?

The standard answer is credibility: a new government, a new central banker, a hard issuance cap, and a visible break with the old regime. But that answer only pushes the question back one step. Credibility is not a substance you can measure. It is a shared belief that emerges from institutions, constraints, and signals.

A century later, every debate about money printing eventually reaches Weimar. The comparison is usually too loose. Modern central banks do not finance government deficits by running the printing press to pay striking workers in an occupied industrial region. But the deeper question Weimar raises is still live: what makes a currency credible, and what makes people stop believing in it?

The mark did not collapse because Germany ran out of paper. It collapsed because Germans ran out of reasons to hold the paper. That is the part of the story that never ages.
