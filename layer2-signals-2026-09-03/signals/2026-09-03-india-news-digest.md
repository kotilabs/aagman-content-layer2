#### 1. Manufacturing PMI hits a five-year low — and jobs just rolled over
- **Topic title:** HSBC India Manufacturing PMI falls to 52.8: the goods economy is losing momentum while services carries the load
- **What happened:** The HSBC India Manufacturing PMI fell to 52.8 in August from 53.5 in July — the weakest reading in five years. Output and new orders grew at their slowest rates since August 2021, and manufacturing employment contracted for the first time in two-and-a-half years. Finished-goods inventories rose for a second month as sales undershot expectations.
- **Why this matters now:** This is the third consecutive monthly decline, landing two days after MoSPI printed 7.8% real GDP growth for Q1 FY27. The highest-frequency private-sector gauge of the goods economy is now pointing the opposite way from the headline growth number, and the jobs component flipping negative is the part that transmits into consumption and credit.
- **Companies, sectors, or indices most affected:** Industrials, capital goods, auto ancillaries, staffing/quarterly hiring commentary, Nifty Midcap industrials; banks with manufacturing working-capital exposure.
- **What most quick commentary is missing:** The disinflation buried inside the weak print. Input cost inflation is the weakest in six months and output charge inflation the slowest in 45 months — a PMI this soft with pricing power this absent reads as a demand problem, not a cost problem. Also: business expectations actually rose to a three-month high, so firms are betting the slowdown is temporary. That gap between current orders and future confidence is the tell.
- **Editorial wedge:** Macro Data / Policy Transmission

#### 2. GST at ₹1.99 lakh crore: the buoyancy is coming from imports, not domestic demand
- **Topic title:** August GST collections rise 14.8% — but imports grew 29% while domestic revenue grew 9.3%
- **What happened:** Gross GST collections for August came in at ₹1.99 lakh crore, up 14.8% YoY. Underneath: revenue from imports jumped 29% to ₹62,604 crore while domestic transaction revenue rose just 9.3% to ₹1.37 lakh crore. Refunds surged 68% to ₹31,795 crore, so net revenue grew only 8.3% to ₹1.68 lakh crore.
- **Why this matters now:** The headline print will be read as a consumption-strength signal alongside the 7.8% GDP print. The composition says something different: roughly 55% of the incremental gross collection came from imports (per AKM Global), the August 31 statutory time-barring deadline pulled forward compliance revenue, and GST buoyancy is running below 1 at ~0.7 (per Tax Connect). Net growth in single digits is a fiscal math issue for the FY27 run-rate, not a demand boom.
- **Companies, sectors, or indices most affected:** Fiscal deficit math, bond supply expectations, consumer discretionary (domestic demand proxy), import-heavy sectors (electronics, capital goods), logistics and ports.
- **What most quick commentary is missing:** The one-off compliance tailwind (time-barring deadline) and the refund surge — refunds growing 68% while gross grows 14.8% means the government's usable revenue is growing much slower than the headline. Read it with the PMI print: domestic GST +9.3% corroborates the manufacturing slowdown more than it contradicts it.
- **Editorial wedge:** Macro Data / Policy Transmission

#### 3. The 2.6% GDP row: India's base-year revision is now a political and statistical fight
- **Topic title:** Garg's "real growth is 2.6%" claim vs MoSPI's new 2022-23 series: how to read 7.8%
- **What happened:** Former finance secretary Subhash Chandra Garg argued India's Q1 FY27 growth was 2.6%, not the officially reported 7.8%, by comparing new-series nominal GDP (₹88.27 lakh crore) with the old 2011-12-series Q1 FY26 figure (₹86.05 lakh crore). The Congress amplified the claim as proof of fudged data. Government sources called it "statistically meaningless" — a new-series numerator on an old-series denominator — and NK Singh (15th Finance Commission chairman) publicly backed the revision on September 2, calling cross-series comparison "apples-to-oranges."
- **Why this matters now:** The new 2022-23 base-year series revised Q1 FY26 nominal GDP down to ₹80 lakh crore from ₹86.05 lakh crore — a ~7% downward level revision that changes every growth denominator in the economy. With the series freshly released (MoSPI, August 31) and immediately politicized, every investor now has to re-anchor what "7.8% growth" means before the market prices earnings and fiscal assumptions off it.
- **Companies, sectors, or indices most affected:** Macro-sensitive positioning across the board; rate expectations (real vs nominal growth feeds RBI's reaction function); PSU/fiscal-linked trades via revised deficit ratios.
- **What most quick commentary is missing:** Two technical points the government put on record that neither side's partisans are engaging with: (a) the statistical discrepancy swung from +₹1.37 lakh crore (Q1 FY26) to −₹1.06 lakh crore (Q1 FY27), which *subtracted* ~3.2 percentage points from headline growth — so the claim that discrepancies manufactured the 7.8% has the sign backwards; (b) the implied GDP deflator is only ~2.3%, and manufacturing now uses double deflation, which can produce negative implicit GVA deflators during commodity shocks. The real debate is about the deflator, not the headline.
- **Editorial wedge:** Macro Data / Policy Transmission (with a narrative-deconstruction edge)
- **Sources:**

#### 4. Brent back at $95+: the India pass-through trade has restarted
- **Topic title:** US airstrikes on Iran push Brent to $95 — what changed since late August, and who pays
- **What happened:** The US carried out a series of airstrikes against Iran on Tuesday (September 1), drawing retaliation from Tehran, and oil rallied for a third consecutive session. Brent settled up 0.4% at $95.13 on September 2 — the highest closes since late July. Indian equities reacted immediately: Sensex fell 373.93 points to 76,570.35 and Nifty lost 141.35 points to close at 23,914.45, with Nifty Auto, Media and IT leading losses while Oil & Gas, Realty and PSU Banks held up. The rupee slipped to 94.97/$.
- **Why this matters now — what changed since late August:** As recently as the August 31 pre-market, *falling* crude was the relief story supporting Nifty above 24,150. Three sessions of escalation have flipped that: crude is once again the dominant input to Indian equity pricing, and it arrives exactly as markets price ~67% odds of a September Fed *hike* — an oil-plus-rates pincer India hasn't faced since the spring escalation.
- **Companies, sectors, or indices most affected:** OMCs (IOC, BPCL, HPCL — CLSA flags them as the big losers), upstream (ONGC, Oil India — CLSA's preferred side), paints (Asian Paints was the top Sensex laggard), aviation, tyres, chemicals, logistics; CPI/WPI pass-through and RBI's "higher headline inflation" warning from the August policy.
- **What most quick commentary is missing:** CLSA's supply math, carried in BusinessLine on September 2: ~70% of the 1+ billion-barrel Hormuz-country supply shortfall from March–July was met by strategic reserve releases from China, the US and the OECD. Those reserves are now severely depleted; CLSA sees limited near-term supply relief and a risk that China/Korea/Japan stop drawing and start importing, driving prices higher over the next two to three months. The curve's backwardation and below-average net-long positioning suggest the market is underpricing exactly that.

#### 5. India's 10-year crosses 7% while the RBI sits at 5.25%
- **Topic title:** Global bond rout plus oil rally batters Indian bonds — the 7% yield problem
- **What happened:** Indian government bonds sold off on September 2, driving the 10-year yield above 7% (from ~6.85% in late August), caught between a global debt rout — US, German and Japanese borrowing costs at or near multi-decade peaks — and the oil rally. Markets are simultaneously pricing a ~67% probability of a 25bp Fed hike at the September 15–16 meeting.
- **Why this matters now:** A 175+ bp spread of the 10-year over the repo rate, with the RBI on an extended hold, means the market is pricing duration and inflation risk the MPC has not acknowledged. Every basis point here transmits into corporate borrowing costs, PSU bank treasury books, and the equity risk premium at a moment when Nifty is already below 24,000.
- **Companies, sectors, or indices most affected:** Banks (treasury gains reversing — note HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank both sold off September 2), NBFC funding costs (Capri Global priced a $300 mn dollar bond at a 7.55% coupon the same day), rate-sensitives (realty, autos via loan rates), PSU bond supply.

The drivers are US Treasury supply (debt past $40 trillion), Japanese yields at multi-decade highs dragging JGB money home, and oil. The interesting India-specific twist: diaspora deposits have crossed $100 billion and reserves are at a record $729.3 billion, so the rupee is defended while bonds are not. Currency stability with rising yields is an unusual configuration — it means the RBI is choosing where to spend its credibility.

## Historical or Analog Triggers

- **The 2015 GDP base-year revision (move to the 2011-12 series):** When India last rebased GDP and changed methodology (GVA at basic prices, market-price GDP, new deflators), measured growth jumped and triggered a multi-year "is the data real?" debate — including credible economists, not just partisans. The episode is the right frame for today's 2022-23 series row: level revisions change denominators for years, and the substantive argument ends up being about deflators and discrepancies, not the headline.
