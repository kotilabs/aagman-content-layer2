# India's Ozempic Moment, Six Months In

## Semaglutide's patent expired in March and the price collapsed overnight. Six months on, the interesting story is who ends up owning the infrastructure around the drug.

Think of a popular hill-station café that alone holds the licence to a famous baker's sourdough recipe. For years, it charges what it likes, and only the tourists who can afford it ever taste the bread. Then the licence expires. Within a week, ten bakeries in town are selling the same loaf at a tenth of the price. Queues form. Two bakeries burn their first batches and have to recall them. The flour merchant — the only one whose yeast the municipal authority has approved — quietly becomes the most important man in the story. And the people making the real, durable money are not baking at all. They are the ones who build the ovens.

That is roughly where India's semaglutide market stands in September 2026, six months after Novo Nordisk's patent expired on 20 March.

### Context

Semaglutide — the molecule sold as Ozempic for diabetes and Wegovy for weight loss — is the defining drug class of this decade. India was the first large market in the world where it faced full generic competition, and the industry did not wait. Within a week of expiry, thirteen companies had launched twenty-six brands. Within a month, more than fifty were on the market or in the queue. Natco opened at ₹1,290 a month for a multi-dose vial — against ₹8,800–11,175 for branded Ozempic and ₹10,850–16,400 for Wegovy before the cliff.

Novo did not retreat. On 1 April it cut starting-dose prices 36% on Ozempic and 48% on Wegovy, bringing both to ₹5,660 a month. The result, in the first full month of data, surprised anyone expecting a rout: according to Pharmarack data reported by Bloomberg, the total GLP-1 market grew 56% month-on-month to 414,000 units in April — and Novo's own volumes *rose* 40% to 32,000 units. Generics expanded the market more than they eroded the innovator. Meanwhile Eli Lilly, whose Mounjaro is still on patent in India until at least 2030, stayed the top-selling GLP-1 provider even as its sales dipped under the price war.

The launch week also produced an instructive shortage. Of the roughly ten brands live at expiry, only three or four were still available online days later — the rest had sold out, on what Jefferies described as inaccurate demand forecasts, thin online allocation, and a supply chain snarled by regulatory crackdowns on unauthorised sale. This, in a country where GLP-1 penetration is an estimated 5% of diabetics and 4% of people with obesity. Demand was never the constraint. Supply was.

### Mechanism

Follow the cost stack, because the ground moved underneath it.

Semaglutide is a peptide — a chain of 31 amino acids, fiddly to make, but at industrial scale closer to a small molecule than a biologic. Chinese manufacturers spent years building multi-tonne capacity ahead of the global patent expiries. The result, per the Economic Times in April: synthetic semaglutide API fell from roughly $900 per gram to $90–160, and fermentation-derived variants to around $50–60, with another 20–30% of softening expected. When the molecule's input cost falls 85%, the molecule stops being where the margin lives.

Margin migrates to whatever is still scarce. Three things qualify. First, sterile fill-finish — loading drug into pen cartridges on isolator-based lines. There are perhaps three or four such facilities in India. Second, the device. A once-weekly injection taken for years lives or dies on the pen, which costs three to six times more to make than a vial and determines whether patients actually stay on therapy. Third, regulatory track record — the thing July's news made vivid.

As of April, exactly one API supplier — Sinopep-Allsino of Hangzhou — held DCGI registration to supply semaglutide API into India. The entire price-collapse story rests on continued Chinese multi-tonne supply at collapsed prices. Domestic entrants (MSN, Apothecon and others) are coming, but the market today runs through a single registered foreign supplier.

Three deviations from the expected script stand out. The innovator grew volumes after cutting price — India's post-cliff dynamic is expansion, not erosion, so far. The early leader among generics, Torrent, won not on price but by launching the only oral version (formulations were never patent-covered; only the molecule was). And the companies with the cleanest economics launched nothing at all: OneSource's customer book — holding roughly two-thirds of the generic market by value — is full and closed, on take-or-pay contracts and capacity-reservation fees.

### Interpretation spectrum

**Reading A: the classic Indian generics story — value migrates from molecule to infrastructure.** The API is commoditised, so the durable economics sit in fill-finish, devices, and peptide intermediates. This is what OneSource's closed order book, Gland's 140 million cartridges, Zydus's licensed pen, and Divi's refusal to touch generics all point to. The weakness in this reading: every bottleneck in Indian pharma has eventually been competed away, and fill-finish capacity is being built at 5x multiples on demand that has not yet printed a full year.

**Reading B: the domestic brand fight is a bloodbath; the real money is export.** Management teams say this themselves — Natco's CEO has said plainly he does not expect to make much money in India, and Dr Reddy's is aiming at 87 countries in 2026, with Canada and Brazil already approving generics. The weakness: export conversion is slipping. Canada's review cycle dragged for months, and Dr Reddy's hit an out-of-spec API problem in July that keeps supplies disrupted until late October or early November. "Abroad" pays later than the slide decks assumed.

**Reading C: the innovator is not dead.** Novo's April volumes rose at ₹5,660; Lilly still tops the market; and both innovators have local partners (Emcure sells Novo's molecule as Poviztra; Cipla sells Lilly's as Yurpeak). For a chronic injectable, brand trust and trial depth matter to prescribers. The weakness: six months is early. Innovator resilience in month two historically says little about year three, when fifty brands have detailing forces in every clinic.

**Reading D: the binding risks are regulatory and geopolitical, not commercial.** July brought voluntary recalls at three companies. The whole market runs on one registered Chinese API supplier. And on 29 September 2026 — later this month — the US Section 232 regime fully phases in: 100% tariffs on patented pharmaceuticals and APIs, with generics exempt for now but under a formal one-year review. That exempts India's generic engine while taxing exactly the innovator-side custom-synthesis work Indian CDMOs covet most. The weakness in this reading is that risk-framing over-weights tails; the base case is still a functioning, expanding market.

**Aagman's structural bias, declared:** we hold a long-cycle view that India's internal demand and manufacturing depth compound through episodes like this. That bias makes Reading A more interesting to us. It is a bias, not a conclusion — the July recalls and the single-supplier API chain are exactly the kind of data that should challenge it.

### Historical or structural memory

India has converted a breakthrough therapy from luxury to commodity before. In 2001, Cipla offered HIV triple therapy at roughly $350 per patient-year against $10,000–15,000 branded — and the durable economics accrued for a decade to whoever mastered production, not whoever held the brand. The difference now: that was a donor-funded procurement market, while GLP-1 in India is a retail, out-of-pocket, lifestyle-adjacent market — with an innovator fighting back on price rather than retreating.

A quieter analogue cuts the other way. Liraglutide, an older GLP-1, went off patent in India earlier — and its leading generic reportedly does about ₹1 crore a month. A generic launch alone does not create a market. Semaglutide's expiry arrived into global cultural salience and a pre-built ₹1,400-crore market; that timing is part of the outcome, and it will not generalise to every molecule.

### Implications for decision-makers

**What this affects:**

- **How you read pharma earnings.** A company "in GLP-1" can mean five different businesses — branded generic, innovator licensee, device maker, fill-finish CDMO, peptide intermediates supplier — with entirely different margin structures and risk. The label is useless; the position in the stack is everything.
- **Where execution risk hides.** This is a peptide at industrial scale. July's recalls show that launching first and manufacturing reliably are different skills. Validated capacity is a moat precisely because it is boring.
- **The supply chain's political exposure.** One registered API supplier, in China, feeding a market whose prices assume continued collapse. Any bilateral friction reprices the whole domestic story upward.
- **The policy clock.** The Section 232 generics exemption is due for review within a year. India's generic exports are shielded today; the innovator-side CDMO work is not. Watch the review, not the headline.

**What this does not affect:**

- The global trajectory of the drug class. The US and EU patents run into 2031–32; India is the preview, not the main event.
- Anything resembling a trade. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell any security.

**What is known, unknown, and unknowable:**

- **Known:** the price collapse happened (₹1,290 vs ₹8,800); the market expanded rather than eroded in month one; API costs fell ~85%; three companies recalled batches in July; fill-finish is the scarce layer; one Chinese supplier holds the registration that matters.
- **Unknown:** the first full post-launch quarter's company-level numbers; whether the recalls were teething trouble or systemic; when a second DCGI-registered API supplier arrives; how long Novo's ₹5,660 defence holds; whether the market pivots oral — oral semaglutide's ~1% bioavailability implies roughly 100x the API per patient (a derived, approximate figure), which would multiply every dependency above.
- **Unknowable:** the geopolitical tail risk on Chinese API supply; whether Indian patients persist on a weekly injection for years at any price; the market's terminal structure — three or four profitable brands, or a permanently fragmented shelf.

### Open question

The easy trade was the scarcity trade, and it ended on 20 March. What remains is the scale trade, and scale trades are settled by whoever owns the bottleneck after the commodity has been competed to cost. Today the bottlenecks are sterile fill-finish lines, a licensed pen, and a single approved supplier in Hangzhou.

The question to sit with is not which brand wins the ₹1,290 shelf war. It is this: when the second DCGI-registered API supplier finally arrives, the bottleneck dissolves — and the whole stack reprices again. Which layer of India's GLP-1 build-out is still scarce the day after that happens?
